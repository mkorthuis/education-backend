# Merrimack Elementary Schools and the Early Years

Part of the Merrimack research (town/Merrimack). Gathered November 15, 2026.

## Summary

This file covers the Merrimack School District's three preschool-to-grade-4 schools (James Mastricola Elementary, Reeds Ferry and Thorntons Ferry), James Mastricola Upper Elementary School (JMUES, grades 5–6) and the Merrimack Early Education Program (MEEP): full-day kindergarten and its history; before- and after-school care (the Nashua Adult Learning Center's School Age Adventures inside the schools, the town's Naticook After School Program, the YMCA and private providers); test results next to Bedford and Nashua; the grades 5–6 school and what research says about school transitions; class-size policy, the 2026-27 staffing reductions and the reinstated grade 4 teacher at Mastricola Elementary; MEEP evaluations, typical peers and tuition; the reading curriculum, screening (including Thorntons Ferry's Acadience screening) and Title I reading help; ESOL; art, music, PE and library specialists; devices and screen time; and teachers' planning time and classroom supply budgets.

- **13 topics, 74 facts and 2 number tables.** Raw copies are in `raw/elementary/` (about 13 MB): district and school pages, the 2026-27 Reeds Ferry and JMUES handbooks, board policies IIB, IHBBA, IHBDA and IMAH, the MTA 2026-2029 teacher contract, ten sets of school board minutes (`raw/elementary/minutes/`), the FY2027 proposed reductions and the Mastricola Elementary and Reeds Ferry budget books, the 2025-26 literacy assessment calendar, the 2026-27 Title I plans for Mastricola Elementary, JMUES and Thorntons Ferry, MEEP's handbook, FAQ and Child Find letter, the October 19, 2026 special education overview, voting results for 2018 and 2026, Parks & Recreation, YMCA and Adult Learning Center pages, two Education Next research articles, one news article, and two read-only queries of the site's database.
- **Topics:** eleven shared slugs (`district-preschool`, `district-school-day-and-calendar`, `town-recreation-and-summer`, `district-school-results-compared`, `district-classrooms-and-staff`, `district-curriculum-and-assessment`, `district-student-support`, `district-enrichment-and-advanced-learners`, `district-pe-outdoor-and-athletics`, `district-technology-and-phones`, `district-budget-and-cost`), the library topic `kindergarten-day-length` with its own kind and description, and **one proposed new shared topic, `research-school-transitions`** (kind research, question type research; "What Research Says About Changing Schools in the Middle Grades": what studies find about student achievement and attendance when students move to a new school in the middle grades, and what they leave out). ## research-school-transitions: What Research Says About Changing Schools in the Middle Grades

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What studies find about student achievement and attendance when students move to a new school in the middle grades, and what they leave out

1. A study of New York City students followed from grade 3 to grade 8 (1998-99 to 2007-08) found that students' test scores in math and English fell in the year they moved from an elementary school to a middle school, compared with similar students who stayed in a K–8 school, and kept falling through the middle school years.
   - citation: Rockoff, J. E., and Lockwood, B. B. (2010). "Stuck in the Middle: How and why middle schools harm student achievement." Education Next, Fall 2010.
   - source: https://www.educationnext.org/stuck-in-the-middle/
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text: "in the specific year when students move to a middle school (or to a junior high), their academic achievement, as measured by standardized tests, falls substantially in both math and English relative to that of their counterparts who continue to attend a K–8 elementary school. What's more, their achievement continues to decline throughout middle school." The authors also found students missed almost two more days of school a year, and that per-pupil spending and class size did not explain the gap; larger grade cohorts may play a part. Neither study in this topic looked at a grades 5–6 school or at New Hampshire. Raw: raw/elementary/ednext-stuck-in-the-middle.txt

2. A study of all Florida public school students in grades 3 to 10 (2000 to 2009) found that moving to a middle school in grade 6 lowered math scores by 0.12 standard deviations and reading by 0.09 compared with students in K–8 schools, that moving in grade 7 lowered them by 0.22 and 0.15, and that the gap grew over the middle school years.
   - citation: West, M. R., and Schwerdt, G. (2012). "The Middle School Plunge: Achievement tumbles when young students change schools." Education Next, 12(2), 63-68.
   - source: https://www.educationnext.org/the-middle-school-plunge/
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text: "we find math achievement falls by 0.12 standard deviations and reading achievement falls by 0.09 standard deviations for transitions at grade 6 ... Students who make the transition at grade 7 experience even larger drops ... of 0.22 and 0.15"; "After three years in a middle school, students who entered in 6th grade score 0.23 standard deviations in math and 0.14 standard deviations in reading worse than we would have expected". A standard deviation is a measure of spread; 0.1 is a small-to-moderate change. Students whose grade 3 scores were below the state median had larger math drops. Raw: raw/elementary/ednext-middle-school-plunge.txt

3. The Florida study found a smaller drop when students moved to high school in grade 9 (0.03 standard deviations in math and 0.04 in reading) that did not last beyond grade 9, and its authors suggested that moving from one school to another, or being in a school's youngest grade, can lower performance; the New York City authors found no evidence for the youngest-grade explanation in their data.
   - citation: West and Schwerdt (2012), Education Next; Rockoff and Lockwood (2010), Education Next
   - source: https://www.educationnext.org/the-middle-school-plunge/
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Florida text: "students moving to a new high school between grades 8 and 9 suffer a small drop in achievement of 0.03 standard deviations in math and 0.04 standard deviations in reading"; "moving from one school to another (or simply being in the youngest grade in a school) adversely affects student performance." New York text: "does being the youngest students in a school have negative effects ...? We could not find evidence in our data to support this explanation for the initial drop". Raw: raw/elementary/ednext-middle-school-plunge.txt; raw/elementary/ednext-stuck-in-the-middle.txt

4. Both the New York City and Florida studies compared stand-alone middle schools with K–8 schools in large systems; neither measured a school serving only grades 5 and 6, and neither can say how one New Hampshire district's students fare when they change schools.
   - citation: Rockoff and Lockwood (2010) and West and Schwerdt (2012), Education Next
   - source: https://www.educationnext.org/stuck-in-the-middle/
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: New York text: "these differences might mean that middle-school attendance would have smaller or larger effects on other students than we estimate it to have on New York City's public school children." Florida grouped students by urban, suburban and rural settings and still found drops. A district's own growth data (as in district-school-results-compared) is the closest local measure, and it too can't separate a transition's effect from other causes. Raw: raw/elementary/ednext-stuck-in-the-middle.txt; raw/elementary/ednext-middle-school-plunge.txt
