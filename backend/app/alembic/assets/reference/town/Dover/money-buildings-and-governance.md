# Dover: Money, Buildings and Governance

Part of the Dover research (town/Dover). Gathered November 15, 2026.

## Summary

**What's covered:** 7 topics (one proposed), 77 facts and 4 number tables on how Dover's school budget is set under the city charter's tax cap, what drove the 2026-27 (FY2027) budget, the three union contracts, Question 2's local effect, the school buildings and bonds, how residents take part, and housing, rents and students per housing unit.
- **The FY2027 budget from start to finish.** The School Board's six workshops (November 17, 2025 to January 12, 2026), the tax-cap scenarios with their staff cuts, the board's budget $1,976,767 over the school part of the cap, the City Manager's February proposal ($877,273 over the overall cap), and the City Council's 9–0 adoption on April 1, 2026, $140,161 under the cap after moving $1,831,515 of downtown TIF money to the schools. Cost drivers: wages and benefits (+$5,285,447), debt service (+$1,909,571), special education transportation (+$527,821), the SchoolCare assessment and switch to Cigna.
- **The charter tax cap:** the city's formula (prior levy × 3-year Boston-area CPI, plus new construction × prior tax rate), the two-thirds vote needed to exceed it, and the override history FY2009–FY2026 (overrides in 9 of 18 years; school portion over in all of them).
- **Where the money goes:** the district's staff mix (29 administrators of 568 staff, 20 of them in schools), the function budget, a **derived** DOE-25 split against the state, and the district's comparison of top administrator pay per student.
- **Special education transportation** line by line, FY2024–FY2027, with the $400,000 added "to cover FY26 overages."
- **Contracts:** the teachers' one-year deal (2026–27), the paraeducators' three-year deal (2026–2029) and the office staff's two-year deal (2026–2028), with council votes and cost summaries.
- **Taxes:** projected FY2027 rate and bills, the tax-cap versus over-cap bill difference, the city's "$100,000 = about $8" rule, annual reappraisal, the elderly exemption.
- **Buildings and bonds:** the new Dover High/CTC ($87.6 million, occupied 2018, $73.9 million bond to 2041), the athletic complex, Dover Middle's geothermal HVAC, and the proposed FY2028–2033 CIP: Woodman Park's balcony ($900,000) and Garrison's $16.5 million renovation; debt limits.
- **Governance and input:** board members and officers, policy BEDH (public comment), the Joint Fiscal Committee, joint meetings, budget hearings, the December 14, 2026 FY2028 hearing.
- **Housing:** rents (NH Housing survey), renters' cost burden, the housing committee's report, and NH Housing's 2024 study of students per housing unit, with Dover's own ratios.

**Main sources:** SAU 11's FY2027 budget book (November 24, 2025), workshop slides (November 17, December 1 and 8, 2025, January 6 and 12, 2026), the three collective bargaining agreements, policies BEDH, FBB and FAA, and School Board minutes (October 5, October 26, November 9, 2026). The City of Dover's FY2027 budget summary (City Manager's letter), Budget Revealed documents (tax cap calculation, override history, adjustments, presentations), City Council minutes and resolutions (publicrecords.dover.nh.gov), the proposed FY2028–2033 CIP, the high school project pages, the elderly exemption page and city news posts. NH Housing's *From Homes to Classrooms* (2024) and 2024 Residential Rental Cost Survey, the city housing committee's final report (2025), and the site's database (Question 2, enrollment, DOE-25).

The 2024 New Hampshire Housing study estimated that housing built from 2014 to 2023 in the four communities brought local school districts a net fiscal benefit averaging $1,711 per unit and older housing $190, with denser condos and apartments yielding far more per acre than single-family homes, while the two small towns whose new homes were mostly single-family (Deerfield and Dunbarton) had net losses on new units.
   - citation: New Hampshire Housing, "From Homes to Classrooms" (September 2024), Detailed Findings 5–11, p. 4
