# Laconia: Money, Taxes, Staff and Buildings

Part of the Laconia research (town/Laconia). Gathered November 16, 2026.

## Summary

**What's covered:** 7 topics, 78 facts and 4 number tables on how Laconia's 2026-27 (FY2027) school budget was built and adopted inside the city budget, the city charter's tax cap and the council's exclusions, what drove the budget (contracts, health insurance, the bus contract, special education and falling revenue), the five teaching positions left unfilled, staffing and teachers' contract terms, state aid and special education aid, the school buildings and the capital plan, and why Laconia's school tax rate is higher than the lake towns'.
- **The FY2027 budget:** the board's three options (March 2026), the $46,237,960 general fund "up 4.4%" (May 5, 2026), the council presentation (May 11), the city's MS-24 school table, the 9.78% rise in school taxes as non-tax revenue fell, and "about 90%" of the increase being contractual.
- **The tax cap:** the 2005 charter amendment, the FY2027 cap arithmetic, the 6-0 council vote on June 22, 2026 to exclude $2,634,559 of debt service, the FY2025–FY2027 record (table), the effect on the rate (**derived**), councilors' comments, and Question 2's state cap (**derived**).
- **Staff and pay:** five vacated teaching positions not refilled plus six support staff changes, the summer 2026 hiring, the vacant high school math post, the teachers' contract (90% of the health premium paid by the district, prep periods, reopener), the 16.9% health insurance rise and the separate $874,867.47 SchoolCare assessment.
- **Where the money goes:** a **derived** DOE-25 split against the state (table).
- **Special education:** the district's own figures (389 of 1,764 students; 24 out-of-district placements), the Lakes Region consortium study, and catastrophic aid as a share of special education spending (**derived**).
- **Buildings:** the renovated LHS locker rooms (April 2025), the LHS project and reserve money, the closed stadium bathrooms, the FY2028–2033 school capital plan submission (turf, stadium drainage and fencing, $13 million of dehumidification, an access road, a new SAU office), the Huot boilers and the school capital reserve's balance, and school debt.
- **Taxes and the lake towns:** the FY2027 rate estimate, billing dates, and a **derived** table of property value and school tax per resident student in Laconia and seven Lake Winnipesaukee towns.
- **Research:** a study of how online school ratings changed home values and who moved.

**Main sources:** the City of Laconia's approved FY2027 budget (with the school's MS-24 table, tax cap analysis, debt schedule and Resolution 2026-07) and the FY2025 and FY2026 budget books; City Council minutes (May 11, June 18, June 22, September 14, 2026); Laconia School Board minutes (October 2025 to October 2026) and its Budget & Personnel and Facilities committee minutes; the 2025-2028 teachers' agreement; the city's FY2028–2033 capital plan submission for the schools; the city's budget and tax pages; Pleasant Street School's staff directory; the site's database (DOE-25, school taxes, enrollment, Question 2); and Hasan and Kumar (2018).

## research-schools-and-home-values: What Research Says About Schools and Home Prices

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What studies find about how school spending, school buildings and school results relate to home prices (added from the Manchester research)

1. A study of the staged rollout of GreatSchools.org ratings across U.S. zip codes from 2006 to 2015 found that once ratings were online, home values in zip codes two standard deviations apart in school performance diverged by an additional $2,249 after one year and $8,996 after four years, in the authors' most conservative models.
   - citation: Sharique Hasan and Anuj Kumar, "Digitization and Divergence: Online School Ratings and Segregation in America," working paper, October 22, 2018, abstract and pp. 2–3
   - source: https://site.warrington.ufl.edu/kumar/files/2018/11/Digitization_and_Divergence__Paper.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-16
   - review_by: 2027-11-16
   - note: Text: "We leverage the staged rollout of GreatSchools.org school ratings from 2006-2015 ... housing prices for zip codes that are 2-sd apart in school performance diverge by an additional $2,249 after 1 year of rating availability and $8,996 after four years of availability." A version was later published in Strategy Science (2024; the journal page refused automated requests). Raw: raw/money/hasan-kumar-2018-digitization-and-divergence.pdf/.txt
2. The same GreatSchools study found that online ratings sped up differences between communities in income, education and racial and ethnic makeup because "Affluent and more educated families were better positioned" to use the ratings to move; it measured home values and who lived in each zip code, and did not report effects on rents or renters.
   - citation: Hasan and Kumar, "Digitization and Divergence," 2018, abstract and p. 3
   - source: https://site.warrington.ufl.edu/kumar/files/2018/11/Digitization_and_Divergence__Paper.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-16
   - review_by: 2027-11-16
   - note: Text: "the mass availability of school ratings has accelerated divergence in housing values, income distributions, education levels, as well as the racial and ethnic composition across communities. Affluent and more educated families were better positioned to leverage this new information to capture educational opportunities in communities with the best schools." "we link rating availability to between a 0.11% and a 0.4% change in the proportion of top income earners in a zip code". A text search of the paper finds no mention of rents, renters or tenants.
