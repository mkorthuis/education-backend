# Buildings, Staff and How Concord Compares

Part of the Concord research (town/Concord). Gathered November 15, 2026.

## Summary

**What's covered.** Repairs to the current Rundlett Middle School while its replacement is built on the same site; the district's capital improvement plan, summer projects, trust funds and what it lists without funding (deferred maintenance); boilers, the new HVAC systems at Concord High, Beaver Meadow and Broken Ground, and the 2026 Beaver Meadow humidity and mold problem; custodial staffing, pay and budget lines; substitutes and the only public teacher-absence data; educational assistant (paraprofessional) needs, pay, mentoring and the 14 positions added in October 2026; teacher contracts, starting pay against area rents, and mentoring for new teachers; the district's stated aim to hire a staff that reflects its students, and the state's teacher and Concord's student demographics; research on school buildings and home prices; and home values in Concord, Bow, Hopkinton and Pembroke ZIP codes.

**Counts.** 7 topics (6 shared topics and 1 proposed new topic), 65 facts, 5 number tables.

**Main sources.** The Concord School District's FY27 draft budget (February 2026), budget work session presentations and minutes, Board of Education minutes and packets (January–November 2026), Capital Facilities Committee minutes (2024–2026), the 2024-25 annual report (with the capital improvement plan presented in March 2025), the 2022 Rundlett existing conditions report, the January 2026 strategic plan update, the facilities page and building sheets, and the posted bargaining agreements (teachers, educational assistants, custodians); the federal Civil Rights Data Collection (2017-18, 2020-21, 2021-22 school files); NCES's National Teacher and Principal Survey; NCES Common Core of Data (via the Urban Institute's Education Data Portal); HUD Fair Market Rents; Zillow's ZIP code home value index; NH Ed 505.06; and Neilson and Zimmerman (2014). The Concord Monitor is cited for two facts: the start of construction (with board minutes) and the DEIJ director post, where it was the only source found.

**Main gaps.** No public figure for the total cost of deferred maintenance; the 10-year capital plan for FY27 was not found posted (the latest found is the March 2025 version). No current teacher-absence data exist for Concord or New Hampshire: the federal survey last collected it in 2017-18. No district count of staff by race or ethnicity was found. No count of paraprofessional vacancies through a school year, or of teacher turnover, is published. No mentoring program specific to special educators was found. Nothing measures elementary attendance-zone turnover or move-ins. No study of Concord's schools and employer or family decisions was found. The 2026-27 teacher salary scale under the new contract was not posted. Tax year 2026 rates for Concord, Bow, Hopkinton and Pembroke couldn't be read (DRA blocks scripts).

2. In the New Haven study, each student was assigned to a zoned elementary, middle and high school by address, dividing the district into 25 neighborhoods, and the authors found no evidence that a school project in one neighborhood changed home prices or reading scores in others.
   - citation: Neilson, C. A., & Zimmerman, S. D. (2014), Journal of Public Economics, 120, 18–31, sections 1.1 and 2.3
   - source: https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/77/2021/04/NZ_JPubE.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text: "Each student in the NHPS system is assigned to a zoned elementary, middle, and high school based on his or her address. School assignment triplets partition the District into 25 geographic areas, which we term 'neighborhoods.'" and "we find no evidence of similar spillovers for reading scores or home prices." Raw: raw/staff/neilson-zimmerman-jpube-2014.txt.

3. Studies that compare home prices on either side of school boundaries find that buyers pay more for homes assigned to schools with higher average test scores, but later work found that much of that price gap reflects differences in the income and background of the people living on each side rather than the test scores themselves, as summarized in Neilson and Zimmerman's review of the research.
   - citation: Neilson, C. A., & Zimmerman, S. D. (2014), Journal of Public Economics, 120, 18–31, section 1.2 (describing Black, 1999, Quarterly Journal of Economics, and Bayer, Ferreira and McMillan, 2007, Journal of Political Economy)
   - source: https://spinup-000d1a-wp-offload-media.s3.amazonaws.com/faculty/wp-content/uploads/sites/77/2021/04/NZ_JPubE.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text: "Black (1999) uses discontinuities in the prices of homes on the borders between school districts to estimate the price effects of differences between school-average test scores. Bayer et al. (2007) nests this identification strategy within a model of housing demand and makes the observation that much of the observed price gap is attributable to endogenous socioeconomic segregation along district boundaries, not to test scores per se." The original papers were not read; this is the review's summary. No study of how Concord's schools affect families' or employers' location choices was found. For school building bonds and home prices, see Manchester money-and-governance.md, research-schools-and-home-values fact 1 (Cellini, Ferreira and Rothstein).
