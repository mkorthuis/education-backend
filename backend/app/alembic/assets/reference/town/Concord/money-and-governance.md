# Concord: Money, Taxes and Governance

Part of the Concord research (town/Concord). Gathered November 15, 2026.

## Summary

**What's covered:** 7 topics (one proposed), 72 facts and 4 number tables on how the Concord School District's budget, taxes, middle school bond and state aid fit together, how residents can follow and take part in Board of Education decisions, and the city's property tax relief.
- **The 2026-27 (FY27) budget from start to finish.** The February draft (+9.56%), the posted budget (+0.03%, 37 positions cut), the March 31 preliminary vote ($115,205,146 general fund; $79,595,688 in local taxes) and the October 26 final vote (+$1,055,887 more in local taxes for 14 special education assistants and other items). Why taxes rose while spending stayed nearly flat: non-tax revenue fell about $7.2 million (state adequacy aid, one-time trust transfers, fund balance).
- **Ten years of cost:** the district's own inflation-adjusted cost per pupil (+32% since 2016), average teacher salary (down 1.3% after inflation) and administrator salary totals; a **derived** DOE-25 table of administration spending per pupil (2016–2025) against the state.
- **The middle school's money:** how the $168,777,480 project (about $155.7 million after expected rebates; cost and site facts are in new-middle-school.md) is being borrowed: the March 2026 sale of $75,950,000 in bonds (4.08%), the full 25-year payment schedule, the district's sample tax impacts, and why no state building aid is coming now (NH DOE's January 2026 letter).
- **The tax rate and the 2026 revaluation:** the local school rate's path from $14.81 to a projected $11.01, why a lower rate does not mean a lower bill, abatements, TIF districts and how they affect school taxes, Penacook's separate school taxes.
- **Seniors:** Concord's elderly exemption ($155,000 to $375,000 off assessed value), deferral, hardship help and veterans' credits.
- **Governance:** the charter (nine members, zones, borrowing by two-thirds vote, voter approval needed to move a school or sell land), Policy 136 on public comment and written comments, YouTube and ConcordTV video, the district's "Board of Education Notes", committees and the 2026 ballot.
- **Rents:** what recent studies find about how much of a property tax increase landlords pass on to tenants.

**Main sources:** Concord School District budget, bond and Board of Education documents (sau8.org and its ParentSquare file store), the district charter, NH DOE's January 13, 2026 building aid letter, Hilltop Securities' debt schedule, the City of Concord's assessing, revaluation, TIF and FY2027 budget pages, RSA 162-K:10 and RSA 189:76, two Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia working papers, NHFPI's April 2026 property tax report, and the site's database (DOE-25 lines, read-only).

## money-local-school-tax: The Local School Tax

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: How the local school tax rate covers the rest of the school budget.

1. New Hampshire does not allow different property tax rates for residential and commercial property, as some other states and cities do; within a town or school district, homes, apartments and businesses pay the same rate on their assessed value.
   - citation: New Hampshire Fiscal Policy Institute, "Property Taxes in New Hampshire: How They Work, How They Compare," April 9, 2026, p. 2
   - source: https://nhfpi.org/assets/2026/04/NHFPI-Property-Taxes-in-New-Hampshire_How-They-Work_How-They-Compare_4.9.26.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text (spacing restored): "other states and cities within them can apply different rates to residential and commercial properties. New Hampshire does not permit differences in rates between property types in that manner." The constitutional text (Part II, Article 5) could not be read (nh.gov returned HTTP 403). The second clause is our plain restatement. Raw: raw/money/research/nhfpi-property-taxes-2026-04.pdf/.txt
2. When a New Hampshire city keeps the full captured value of a tax increment finance (TIF) district, that captured value is deducted from the valuation used to set every property tax rate, including the school rate, and the taxes paid on it go to the TIF district's plan; value the plan does not need is "excess captured assessed value" and must be returned to the tax lists.
   - citation: RSA 162-K:10, II(b) and III(a)(1)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XII/162-K/162-K-10.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
   - note: Text: "the full captured assessed value, to be deducted from the current assessed valuation for the calculation of the property tax rate ... the municipal tax collector shall remit to the municipality that proportion of all taxes paid that year on real property in the district which the captured assessed value bears to the total current assessed value"; "Excess captured assessed value shall be returned to the tax lists." Different rules apply to districts that issued TIF bonds before April 29, 1999 (III(b)). Raw: raw/money/rsa/rsa-162-k-10.html/.txt
3. New Hampshire TIF increments may be spent only on the district's development plan, to pay its costs and administrative expenses.
   - citation: RSA 162-K:10, IV
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XII/162-K/162-K-10.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
   - note: Text: "The municipality shall expend the tax increments received for any development program only in accordance with the tax increment financing plan. ## research-property-tax-and-rents: Property Taxes and Rents

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What studies find about how much of a property tax increase landlords pass on to renters, and how fast (proposed from the Concord research)

1. A 2025 Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia working paper using new-tenant rents in Berkeley, California, where Proposition 13 gives similar buildings very different tax bills, found landlords passed through $0.50 to $0.89 of each $1 of property tax increase to new tenants.
   - citation: Sarah Baker, "Property Tax Pass-Through to Renters: A Quasi-Experimental Approach," Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia Working Paper 25-41, December 2025, abstract
   - source: https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/working-papers/2025/wp25-41.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text: "I find strong evidence that landlords faced with quasi-random, building-level property tax shocks pass through $0.50–$0.89 per $1 of the property tax shock to renters." A working paper (not peer reviewed), one city with rent rules of its own. Raw: raw/money/research/philfed-wp25-41.pdf/.txt
2. A 2026 Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia working paper found that after Philadelphia's 2023 reassessment, about 73% of tax increases were passed on to tenants in professionally managed large apartment buildings, starting about three quarters of a year later, but found no significant rent increase across the wider rental market, where smaller landlords passed on less.
   - citation: Lei Ding, Yilin Hou and Sisi Zhang, "Short-Term Incidence of Property Tax Hikes on Professionally Managed Apartments — Evidence from Philadelphia," Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia Working Paper 26-51, November 2026, abstract
   - source: https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/working-papers/2026/wp26-51.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text: "about 73 percent of tax increases were passed through to tenants in professionally managed large-scale apartments, with effects emerging approximately three quarters after the policy shock. However, we find no significant evidence of tax-induced rent increases at the broader market level, likely due to the smaller tax increases faced by most large multifamily properties and weaker cost pass-through among smaller rentals." Working paper. Raw: raw/money/research/philfed-wp26-51.pdf/.txt
3. Earlier studies of how much of the property tax renters bear range from about 15% (Carroll and Yinger, 1994) to 40–50% in more recent work, and the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy uses 50% as its benchmark, according to a 2026 review; the share depends on how easily renters can move, local services and rent rules.
   - citation: Ding, Hou and Zhang, Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia Working Paper 26-51, November 2026, pp. 6–7 (literature review)
   - source: https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/FRBP/Assets/working-papers/2026/wp26-51.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text: "the extent of pass-through varies wildly across the studies and is sensitive to renter mobility, the variation in public services in urban areas, and restrictions on rent increases ... The magnitude of tax incidence ranges from 15% (Carroll and Yinger, 1994) to 40–50% in more recent studies and almost 100% in a study of commercial real estate (Rolheiser, 2019) ... (ITEP) ... uses 50% as benchmark pass-through rate". The cited studies were not read directly. Raw: raw/money/research/philfed-wp26-51.txt
4. The New Hampshire Fiscal Policy Institute writes that property taxes raise housing costs for owners directly and for renters when costs are passed through, but that taxes do not rise directly with assessments and what landlords can charge depends on what renters are willing and able to pay.
   - citation: New Hampshire Fiscal Policy Institute, "Property Taxes in New Hampshire: How They Work, How They Compare," April 9, 2026, p. 17
   - source: https://nhfpi.org/assets/2026/04/NHFPI-Property-Taxes-in-New-Hampshire_How-They-Work_How-They-Compare_4.9.26.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Text (spacing restored): "Property taxes typically increase the cost of housing. They raise the cost of owning a home through direct taxation, and rents increase when costs are passed through from homeowners to renters. However, property taxes do not typically increase directly with assessments, and housing markets are subject to the willingness and ability of homebuyers and renters to pay within a market." Raw: raw/money/research/nhfpi-property-taxes-2026-04.txt
