# School Results and the Community in Manchester

Part of the Manchester research (town/Manchester). Gathered November 15, 2026.

## Summary

**What's covered.** How Manchester's schools compare with each other and with the state on the 2026 state tests: proficiency, growth (mean student growth percentile, which the site stores but the Ask's tools don't show), results by family income and English learner status, state ratings and improvement designations; the one public school-level measure of teacher experience; chronic absenteeism and what the district does about it; how to volunteer, mentor or tutor in the schools and which outside groups do; grade 11 results and readiness measures; how outside rating sites score schools; and research on family income and test scores.

**Counts.** 8 topics (5 existing shared topics and 3 proposed new ones below), 49 facts, 4 number tables.

**Main sources.** NH DOE's public disaggregated assessment files, 2022–2026 (Manchester and state rows saved from the copies the site loads); NH DOE's iExplore school metrics export for 2025 (Manchester rows); the site's database (free and reduced-price meals and truancy by school); the district's volunteer Google Site and volunteer handbook; Big Brothers Big Sisters of NH & VT, the YMCA, City Year and the City of Manchester's volunteer page; GreatSchools' ratings methodology; Stanford's Educational Opportunity Project; Attendance Works' 2025 and 2026 state reviews. Manchester Ink Link is cited for three board presentations because the district's own site (mansd.org) could not be read (see Not retrieved).

**Main gaps.** Every page on mansd.org returns a JavaScript bot check, so district policies, board and committee documents, the 2026 State of the District and the district's own 2026 results article were not read. NH DOE does not publish chronic absence rates, so there are no official rates for Central or West. No school-level teacher turnover figures are published. No source says what reading coaches or interventionists Gossler Park has now.

**Proposed new shared topics.**
- `district-school-results-compared` | Comparing a District's Schools: Scores, Growth and Student Needs | local | comparison | How a district's schools compare on state test proficiency and growth, the share of low-income students and English learners at each, state ratings and improvement designations, and what the comparisons can't tell.
- `school-rating-sites` | How Outside School Rating Sites Score Schools | how_it_works | meaning | How national school rating websites build their ratings from state test scores, growth and college readiness data, and what they leave out.
- `research-family-income-and-test-scores` | What Research Says About Family Income, Test Scores and Growth | research | research | What research finds about how strongly average test scores and learning rates track family income, and why growth can tell a different story from scores.

## school-rating-sites: How Outside School Rating Sites Score Schools

- kind: how_it_works

- description: How national school rating websites build their ratings from state test scores, growth and college readiness data, and what they leave out

1. GreatSchools, a national nonprofit, gives public schools an overall 1–10 rating built from up to three ratings that each compare the school with others in its state: a Student Progress (growth) Rating, a Test Score Rating based on the share of students proficient on state tests, and, for high schools, a College Readiness Rating based on SAT or ACT scores, graduation rates and advanced course participation.
   - citation: GreatSchools, "About GreatSchools ratings" and "GreatSchools Ratings Methodology Report"
   - source: https://www.greatschools.org/gk/about/ratings-methodology/

Text: "Ratings at the lower end of the scale (1–4) signal below-average performance, 5–6 indicate average performance, and 7–10 demonstrate above-average performance."

2. GreatSchools gives its growth rating a base weight of 0.45 when it is higher than the school's test score rating, and gives the test score rating that weight when it is higher, with the other ratings at 0.27, before adjusting for how much data each rating has.
   - citation: GreatSchools Ratings Methodology Report, "Step 1: Establish base weights"
   - source: https://www.greatschools.org/gk/about/ratings-methodology/
   - as_of: 2026-11-15

3. GreatSchools says it places strong emphasis on growth because growth "is less influenced by students' socioeconomic backgrounds" than test scores, and its growth rating uses each state's own growth data, which in New Hampshire is the student growth percentile.
   - citation: GreatSchools, "About GreatSchools ratings," Student (or Academic) Progress Rating
   - source: https://www.greatschools.org/gk/about/ratings/

## research-family-income-and-test-scores: What Research Says About Family Income, Test Scores and Growth

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What research finds about how strongly average test scores and learning rates track family income, and why growth can tell a different story from scores

1. Using test scores from school districts across the United States, Stanford researcher Sean Reardon found that 3rd grade scores are much higher in more affluent districts, but that districts' learning rates from 3rd to 8th grade are only "modestly higher" in more affluent districts, "with many exceptions."
   - citation: Reardon, S. F. (2019), "Affluent Schools Are Not Always the Best Schools," Educational Opportunity Project at Stanford University (SEDA 3.0 data)
   - source: https://edopportunity.org/discoveries/affluent-schools-are-not-always-best/
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Raw: raw/results/research/edopportunity-affluent-schools-are-not-always-best.html (.txt): "On average, learning rates are modestly higher in more affluent districts than in less affluent ones, but the data reveal many exceptions."

2. In the same analysis, Chicago's public schools, whose 3rd graders score below a 2nd-grade level on average, had a learning rate 22% above the national average, while more affluent Anne Arundel County, Maryland had a rate 16% below average, and there was "almost no relationship" between districts' 3rd grade scores and their learning rates.
   - citation: Reardon (2019), "Affluent Schools Are Not Always the Best Schools"
   - source: https://edopportunity.org/discoveries/affluent-schools-are-not-always-best/
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Raw: raw/results/research/edopportunity-affluent-schools-are-not-always-best.txt: "Chicago boasts a learning rate 22 percent above average, while Anne Arundel County has learning rates 16 percent slower than average." The analysis is of districts nationally, not New Hampshire schools.

3. Reardon argues that schools should be judged by how much students learn while enrolled rather than by average scores, comparing it to judging a hospital by how much its patients' health improves, but notes that other factors, such as family resources or after-school programs, may also play a role in learning rates.
   - citation: Reardon (2019), "Affluent Schools Are Not Always the Best Schools"
