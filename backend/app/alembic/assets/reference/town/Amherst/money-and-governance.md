# Money and Governance: The Amherst School District, the Souhegan Cooperative and SAU 39

Part of the Amherst research (town/Amherst). Gathered 2026-11-21.

## Summary

What's covered: the two districts' 2026-27 (FY27) budgets and what drove them, the FY28 drafts now under way, special education spending (in-house staff versus out-of-district tuition), the teachers' and support staff contracts and pay scales, the Mont Vernon middle school tuition agreement, how Souhegan's cost is split between Amherst and Mont Vernon, the 2026 tax rate after the revaluation, the elderly exemption, the Wilkins building votes from 2022 to 2026 and the new 2026 plan, capital reserve funds and the use of year-end surplus, enrollment projections, default budgets, the four boards in SAU 39, the Attorney General's electioneering letter, building aid, open jobs and wages, and purchasing rules.

Counts: 11 topics (10 shared slugs and 1 proposed new slug), 65 facts, 5 number tables.

Main sources: SAU 39's Business Office document folders (voter guides, hearing and deliberative presentations, budget workbooks, election results, audits, NESDEC enrollment reports, contracts, awarded bids), the Wilkins project website, the Town of Amherst's assessing pages and NH DRA's 2026 tax rate sheet, SAU 39's job board, and two news reports for the Attorney General's letter.

Main gaps: the Attorney General's letter itself (only news reports were found); school board minutes on Simbli (blocked by a bot check); Amherst's current state building aid rate (the NH DOE rate sheet returned HTTP 403); whether Amherst's school kitchens buy from local farms; and the SAU 39 board's membership and voting weights.

Proposed new shared topic: `electioneering-by-public-employees` (kind rule, question type rules): "New Hampshire's law on public employees electioneering while on duty or with public property, what counts as electioneering, and the penalties." No existing topic covers it; Amherst's P17.3 needs it, and Pelham's and Goffstown's raw minutes also mention electioneering.

## electioneering-by-public-employees: Electioneering by Public Employees

- kind: rule
- question_type: rules
- description: New Hampshire's law on public employees electioneering while on duty or with public property, what counts as electioneering, and the penalties (proposed from the Amherst research)

1. New Hampshire law bars public employees, including school employees, from electioneering while performing their official duties or using government property or equipment such as phones, vehicles and computers for it; a violation is a misdemeanor, and since January 1, 2026 a person not charged with the misdemeanor may instead face a civil penalty of up to $1,000.
   - citation: RSA 659:44-a
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/LXIII/659/659-44-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-21
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
   - note: "I. No public employee, as defined in RSA 273-A:1, IX,shall electioneer while in the performance of his or her official duties. II. No public employee shall use government property or equipment ... for electioneering ... IV. (a) Any person who violates this section shall be guilty of a misdemeanor. (b) Any person who violates this provision and is not subject to the penalties imposed under subparagraph IV(a) shall be subject to a civil penalty not to exceed $1,000." Source note: "2026, 285:1, 2, eff. Jan. 1, 2026." Raw: raw/money/law/rsa-659-44-a.txt
2. Under New Hampshire law, electioneering means visibly or audibly spreading information that a reasonable person would believe explicitly advocates for or against a candidate, party or measure being voted on, including distributing a handbill, circular or other communication that does so.
   - citation: RSA 652:16-h
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/LXIII/652/652-16-h.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-21
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
   - note: "Electioneering means visibly displaying or audibly disseminating information that a reasonable person would believe explicitly advocates for or against any candidate, political party, or measure being voted. Electioneering includes ... II. Distributing or posting a card, handbill, poster, placard, picture, pin, sticker, circular, or any other form of communication that a reasonable person would believe explicitly advocates for or against any candidate, political party, or measure." ## property-tax-bills-and-escrow: Property Tax Bills, Rate Setting and Mortgage Escrow

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: How and when property tax rates are set and bills issued, and how mortgage escrow payments adjust when taxes change

1. New Hampshire's official assessing manual calls it "sales chasing" when an assessor changes one property's assessment toward its recent sale price without making the same change to similar unsold properties, and treats it as improper, so buying a home does not by itself reset its assessment; values change when a town revalues or updates all properties, or for changes such as new construction.
   - citation: NH Assessing Standards Board, Understanding NH Property Taxes (The Official New Hampshire Assessing Reference Manual), 3rd edition, January 2019, Section 4.2, p. 4-3
   - source: https://www.amherstnh.gov/DocumentCenter/View/4394/Understanding-Nh-Property-Taxes
   - as_of: 2026-11-21
   - review_by: 2027-11-21
   - note: "Sales chasing is the practice of changing an individual property assessment to or near to the recent selling price of that property with the intention of manipulating equalization ratio study results ... When a single property assessment is changed in the direction of the sale price and that same change is not made to other similar unsold properties, the results may not accurately reflect the assessment level of the municipality." Amherst's assessing page: "The assessing department begins inspections for building permits around the end of January ... If the work is not completed prior to April 1, a percentage of the improvement will be placed on the assessment." The manual is posted on Amherst's Assessing page.
