# Getting to School, the School Day and School Zones in Nashua

Part of the Nashua research (town/Nashua). Gathered November 15, 2026.

## Summary

This file covers how Nashua students get to school and how the school day and school zones work: who gets a bus and who walks, the First Student contract and how reliable the buses have been, late buses and bus tracking, buses for charter and private school students, the Academy for Science and Design (ASD) commute, crossing guards, school hours and early drop-off, two-hour delays and snow days, before- and after-school care, high school start times, how students are assigned to schools and can stay at or change schools, zone maps and the 2023–2026 redistricting and facilities plans, school rating sites, and home values by ZIP code.

- **Premises checked:**
  - "Right on the edge of the walk zone" (P3.2): consistent. Kindergarten to grade 5 students get a free bus only if they live more than 1 mile from their assigned school, measured along the most direct traveled route; the superintendent may relax the distance where walking is unsafe.
  - "Are there still driver shortages?" (P5.3): partly. Board records describe "unprecedented" transportation problems in fall 2024 and a "smoother start" in 2025-26, and in March 2026 the district was watching a possible national bus driver strike with no change to service. No 2026-27 shortage was reported in the minutes read (through October 28, 2026). Only one company bid on the 2025 bus contract. No on-time figures are published.
  - "Crossing guards at all the busy intersections" (P8.1): not always. In September 2023, 58% of the district's crossing guard posts were unstaffed; in March 2024, 30%. On November 15, 2026 the district was advertising eight posts plus substitutes. No public list of posts by school was found.
  - "Does the city provide a bus to Nashua charter schools?" (P14.2): the buses are the school district's (run by its contractor, First Student), not the city's. State law requires the district to carry resident charter students on its regular routes; families arrange it through the charter school.
  - "If she gets into ASD" (P11.3): ASD is in Nashua (9 Townsend West, off Amherst Street), and Nashua residents ride district buses there; high schoolers need the $65 bus pass.
  - "Start times at North and South ever going to shift later?" (P35.3): both start at 7:20 a.m. in 2026-27. No proposal to change them was found in Board of Education minutes from 2021 to October 2026. A 2004 committee studied starts of 8:00–8:30 but found it would cost over $1 million.
  - "Which elementary zones have changed recently?" (P29.2): no elementary boundary change was found since 2023, when the board postponed elementary redistricting. Middle school lines changed in 2023–2024 when McCarthy opened. The 2025–2026 Facilities Master Plan includes options that would close one to five elementary schools and redistrict; none had been adopted on November 15, 2026.
  - "Does my son have to switch mid-year?" (P3.3) and "Can he stay at Fairgrounds if his mom moves?" (P30.3): not automatically. The district has a variance process to stay at the school where the year began, with the family driving; a child's school residence follows where the child lives with a parent or court-appointed guardian.
  - "Higher-rated elementary schools in South Nashua drive up home values" (P28.2): the data can't show cause. ZIP 03062 (south Nashua) has a typical home value about $75,000 higher than 03060 (downtown and central), but ZIP codes don't match school zones and homes differ in size and age.

## research-schools-and-home-values: What Research Says About Schools and Home Prices

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What studies find about how school spending, school buildings and school results relate to home prices

1. A study of home sales in three Massachusetts counties (Middlesex, Essex and Norfolk) from 1993 to 1995 compared houses on opposite sides of elementary attendance boundaries within the same district, so that they differed mainly in which school a child would attend, and found parents paid about 2.5% more for a 5% higher school test score, about half the estimate from ordinary price comparisons.
   - citation: Black, Sandra E. (1999), "Do Better Schools Matter? Parental Valuation of Elementary Education," Quarterly Journal of Economics 114(2): 577–599; working paper version, Federal Reserve Bank of New York Research Paper 9729 (1997)
   - source: https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:114:y:1999:i:2:p:577-599.
   - as_of: 2026-11-15
   - review_by: 2027-11-15
   - note: Abstract text: "I look within school districts at houses located on attendance district boundaries; houses then differ only by the elementary school the child attends. I thereby effectively remove the variation in neighborhoods, taxes, and school spending. I find that parents are willing to pay 2.5 percent more for a 5 percent increase in test scores." Working paper: "The data cover all purchases and sales from 1993 through 1995 for Middlesex, Essex, and Norfolk counties in Massachusetts"; it reports "approximately 2.1 %, or $3,948" for the same change and says the boundary estimate "is approximately half the estimate one gets by running a typical hedonic housing price regression." The published figure (2.5%) is used here. Adds to Manchester's topic of this slug (money-and-governance.md). Raw: raw/daily/repec-black-1999-qje.txt; raw/daily/frbny-black-1997-wp9729.txt

## research-school-start-times: What Research Says About Teen Sleep and School Start Times

2. GreatSchools turns a school's growth and test results into 1–10 scores by ranking the school against others in its state and splitting the rankings into tenths (top 10% gets a 10, the next 10% a 9 and so on), so a rating describes a school's position among the state's schools rather than a fixed level of achievement.

   - note: Page text: "Convert each grade/subject growth score into a percentile relative to statewide schools (e.g., a score in the 70th percentile means the school performed better than 70% of schools on that measure) / Average the percentiles across all grade/subject combinations / Convert the average percentile into a 1–10 score using decile bands (top 10% = 10, next 10% = 9, etc.)". The second half of the statement restates what decile ranking means. Raw: raw/daily/greatschools-ratings-methodology.txt
