# Part 1. What the site's numbers mean

## measure-nh-sas-achievement-levels: Achievement Levels on the State Test (NH SAS)

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: The four levels on New Hampshire's state test, what each means, and which count as "proficient."

1. New Hampshire's state test, the New Hampshire Statewide Assessment System (NH SAS), reports every student's score in one of four performance levels: Level 1 Below Proficient, Level 2 Approaching Proficient, Level 3 Proficient and Level 4 Above Proficient.
   - citation: NH DOE Office of Assessment, "NH SAS Performance Levels," August 5, 2024, p. 1
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhsas-cut-scores.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2025-08-31
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: raw/terms/doe/nhsas-cut-scores-2024.pdf
2. NH DOE counts Levels 3 and 4 as proficient (and above proficient) and Levels 1 and 2 as below proficient when it reports the share of students proficient.
   - citation: NH DOE, Assessment Data page (Bureau of Education Statistics)
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/who-we-are/division-of-educator-and-analytic-resources/bureau-of-education-statistics/assessment-data
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-10-21
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. On the NH SAS in English language arts, NH DOE describes a Level 4 student as highly proficient: the student "generally performs significantly above the standard for the grade level, is able to access above-grade-level content, and engages in higher-order thinking skills independently."
   - citation: NH DOE, "NH SAS Performance Levels," August 5, 2024, ELA Performance Level Descriptors
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhsas-cut-scores.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2025-08-31
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. On the NH SAS in English language arts, NH DOE describes a Level 3 (Proficient) student as one who "generally performs at the standard for the grade level, is able to access grade-level content, and engages in higher-order thinking skills with some independence and minimal support."
   - citation: NH DOE, "NH SAS Performance Levels," August 5, 2024, ELA Performance Level Descriptors
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhsas-cut-scores.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2025-08-31
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
5. On the NH SAS in English language arts, NH DOE describes a Level 2 (Approaching Proficient) student as one who "generally performs slightly below the standard for the grade level," and a Level 1 (Below Proficient) student as one who "generally performs significantly below the standard for the grade level." The math descriptors use the same wording pattern.
   - citation: NH DOE, "NH SAS Performance Levels," August 5, 2024, ELA and Mathematics Performance Level Descriptors
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhsas-cut-scores.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2025-08-31
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
6. On the NH SAS, each grade and subject has its own range of scale scores for each level; for example, in grade 5 math a score of 495 to 521 is Proficient (Level 3) and 522 to 660 is Above Proficient (Level 4).
   - citation: NH DOE, "NH SAS Performance Levels," August 5, 2024, Mathematics table
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhsas-cut-scores.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2025-08-31
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
7. NH DOE notes that NH SAS score ranges differ by grade, so the same scale score does not mean the same thing in two different grades.
   - citation: NH DOE, Assessment Data page
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/who-we-are/division-of-educator-and-analytic-resources/bureau-of-education-statistics/assessment-data
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-10-21
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
8. NH DOE's public test results file gives, for each school, district, grade and student group, the percent of students at each of the four levels, the percent at Levels 3 and 4 combined ("Above prof% (lvl 3&4)"), the participation rate, the mean student growth percentile and the average score.
   - citation: NH DOE, 2025-26 Disaggregated Data File (Regular Denominator), column headings
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/2026/publicdisaggregateddata2026_csvformat.csv
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: the copy the site loads is education-backend assets/assessments/assessments-2026.csv; a sample is in raw/terms/doe/iplatform/assessments-2026-sample.csv
9. In NH DOE's regular test results files, the denominator is the number of students tested; for 2017-18 and 2018-19 NH DOE also published files whose denominator is the greater of the students tested or 95% of the students who should have taken the test.
   - citation: NH DOE, Assessment Data page
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/who-we-are/division-of-educator-and-analytic-resources/bureau-of-education-statistics/assessment-data
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-10-21
   - review_by: 2027-11-08

## measure-state-test-grades-and-subjects: Which Grades and Subjects New Hampshire Tests

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: Which students take which state tests, what the law requires, and when parents can opt out.

1. New Hampshire law requires a statewide assessment of mathematics and English language arts in each of grades 3 through 8 and one grade in high school, and a statewide science assessment once in grade 5, once in grade 8 and once in high school, in all public schools, chartered public schools and public academies.
   - citation: RSA 193-C:6, I
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/193-C/193-C-6.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
2. NH DOE gives the NH SAS in English language arts and math in grades 3 to 8, and NH SAS science in grades 5, 8 and the third year of high school.
   - citation: NH DOE Bureau of Assessment & Accountability, Policy and Procedures Manual (updated December 25, 2025), section 1.1
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhdoe-assessment-manual-final.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-08-04
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. New Hampshire law lets a parent or legal guardian exempt a student from the state assessment; the district provides an alternative activity, and the state may not penalize a district for a lower participation rate.
   - citation: RSA 193-C:6, III and VII
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/193-C/193-C-6.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. Under New Hampshire law, a student's state assessment results do not go on the student's transcript unless the student (if 18 or older) or the parent consents.
   - citation: RSA 193-C:6, VI
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/193-C/193-C-6.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
5. Students with the most significant cognitive disabilities take the Dynamic Learning Maps (DLM) alternate assessment instead of the NH SAS, in ELA and math in grades 3 to 8 and the third year of high school, and in science in grades 5, 8 and the third year of high school; the student's IEP team decides using an Alternate Assessment Decision Making Worksheet.
   - citation: NH DOE Assessment Policy and Procedures Manual (updated December 25, 2025), section 1.3
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhdoe-assessment-manual-final.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-08-04
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
6. English learners in New Hampshire take ACCESS, the English language proficiency test, every year in February and March until they meet the state's exit criteria.
   - citation: NH DOE Assessment Policy and Procedures Manual (updated December 25, 2025), section 1.4
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhdoe-assessment-manual-final.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-08-04
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
7. In New Hampshire, English learners who first enrolled in a U.S. school on or after April 1 of the prior year are excluded from one administration of the state ELA test but still take the math and science tests; their math and science results appear on the State Report Card but are not used in the federal accountability indicators.
   - citation: NH DOE Assessment Policy and Procedures Manual (updated December 25, 2025), section 1.4 and table notes
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhdoe-assessment-manual-final.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-08-04
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
8. Federal law lets a state exclude an English learner who has been enrolled in a U.S. school for less than 12 months from one administration of the reading or language arts test.
   - citation: 20 U.S.C. 6311(b)(3)(A) (ESEA section 1111(b)(3)(A))
   - source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/6311
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08

## measure-sat-grade-11-state-test: The SAT as the High School State Test

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: Why the grade 11 results come from the SAT and how SAT scores become "proficient."

1. New Hampshire law allows the Department of Education to use the College Board SAT or the ACT to meet the high school testing requirement.
   - citation: RSA 193-C:6, V
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/193-C/193-C-6.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
2. The New Hampshire SAT School Day with Essay is the state's general assessment in English language arts and math for students in their third year of high school; NH DOE received approval to use the SAT in spring 2016, and the essay is required for all New Hampshire students.
   - citation: NH DOE Assessment Policy and Procedures Manual (updated December 25, 2025), section 1.2
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhdoe-assessment-manual-final.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-08-04
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. NH DOE says that by using the SAT, high school students take it free during the school day, their scores count for state accountability, and they can use the scores for college admission.
   - citation: NH DOE Assessment Policy and Procedures Manual (updated December 25, 2025), section 1.2
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhdoe-assessment-manual-final.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-08-04
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. NH DOE converts each student's SAT section score to one of the same four state levels: in Evidence-Based Reading and Writing, 480 to 620 is Level 3 Proficient and 630 to 800 is Level 4; in Math, 530 to 640 is Level 3 Proficient and 650 to 800 is Level 4.
   - citation: NH DOE Office of Accountability, "Understanding the Achievement Indicator" fact sheet, SAT Scale Score Conversion Chart, p. 3
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/understanding-the-achievement-indicator.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
5. On the same NH DOE chart, SAT scores of 420 to 470 in reading and writing and 420 to 520 in math are Level 2 Approaching Proficient, and 200 to 410 in either is Level 1 Below Proficient.
   - citation: NH DOE, "Understanding the Achievement Indicator" fact sheet, p. 3
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/understanding-the-achievement-indicator.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
6. NH's ESSA state plan uses separate, College Board college-and-career benchmarks (480 in Evidence-Based Reading and Writing and 530 in Math) as one of the ways a 12th grader can count as college- and career-ready.
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised submission February 1, 2023), section A.4.iv.e (College and Career Readiness indicator)
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08

## measure-state-vs-naep-proficient: State "Proficient" vs. NAEP Proficient

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: Why a school's state proficiency rate and New Hampshire's NAEP results measure different things. (NAEP figures themselves are in `national-comparisons.md`.)

1. New Hampshire's "proficient" on the NH SAS means Level 3 or 4 on the state's own test, set against New Hampshire's grade-level standards.
   - citation: NH DOE, Assessment Data page; NH DOE "NH SAS Performance Levels," August 5, 2024
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/who-we-are/division-of-educator-and-analytic-resources/bureau-of-education-statistics/assessment-data
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-10-21
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
2. The National Center for Education Statistics states that "NAEP Proficient does not represent grade-level proficiency as determined by other assessment standards (e.g., state or district assessments)."
   - citation: NCES 2026-014, Mapping State Proficiency Standards Onto the NAEP Scales (February 2026), note to the NAEP achievement level table
   - source: https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/subject/publications/studies/pdf/2026014.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. NCES describes students at or above NAEP Proficient as demonstrating "solid academic performance and competency over challenging subject matter," and notes that NAEP cut scores have not changed since they were set in the early 1990s.
   - citation: NCES 2026-014, February 2026, note to the NAEP achievement level table
   - source: https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/subject/publications/studies/pdf/2026014.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. NAEP is a separate federal test given to a sample of students; NH DOE describes it as the only measure of student achievement comparable across states, with New Hampshire results reported only for the state as a whole.
   - citation: NH DOE Assessment Policy and Procedures Manual (updated December 25, 2025), section 1.5
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/nhdoe-assessment-manual-final.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-08-04
   - review_by: 2027-11-08

## measure-suppressed-and-ranged-results: What "<10%", ">90%", "* n < 11" and Blank Cells Mean

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: How New Hampshire hides small groups and extreme percentages in public data, and the minimum group size of 11.

1. New Hampshire's minimum group size (the "minimum-n") is 11 students: NH DOE says results are not publicly reported for groups smaller than 11 students, to protect student privacy and ensure reliable reporting.
   - citation: NH DOE Office of Accountability, "Federal Accountability System in New Hampshire" fact sheet, p. 1
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/ta_federal-accountability-system-in-new-hampshire.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
2. NH's ESSA state plan sets a minimum-n of 11 for all accountability indicators and says the reporting minimum is the same (it is not lower than the accountability minimum); the plan says New Hampshire has used 11 since its No Child Left Behind system.
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised February 1, 2023), section A.4.ii (Minimum N-Size), pp. 17-18
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. Federal law requires each state to set one minimum number of students for its accountability system, the same for all students and every student group, and to explain how it protects personally identifiable information.
   - citation: 20 U.S.C. 6311(c)(3) (ESEA section 1111(c)(3))
   - source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/6311
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. In NH DOE's 2025-26 test results file, any group with fewer than 11 tested students shows "* n < 11" in place of its percentages, mean growth and average score.
   - citation: NH DOE, 2025-26 Disaggregated Data File (Regular Denominator)
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/2026/publicdisaggregateddata2026_csvformat.csv
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: observed in the file (42,710 rows show "* n < 11"); the file has no written legend
5. In NH DOE's 2025-26 test results file, percentages below 10 are shown only as "< 10 %" and percentages above 90 only as "> 90 %", and the number of students tested is shown as a range of five (for example "70 - 75") rather than an exact count.
   - citation: NH DOE, 2025-26 Disaggregated Data File (Regular Denominator)
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/2026/publicdisaggregateddata2026_csvformat.csv
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: observed in the file; NH DOE's Assessment Data page says only that "the data is suppressed when considering small numbers of students." No published NH DOE rule giving the reason for the 10%/90% ranges was found (see Not retrieved).
6. NH DOE's Assessment Data page states that, "to protect the privacy of students, the data is suppressed when considering small numbers of students."
   - citation: NH DOE, Assessment Data page
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/who-we-are/division-of-educator-and-analytic-resources/bureau-of-education-statistics/assessment-data
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2026-10-21
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
7. NH DOE's 2016 data privacy guide explains "cell size" suppression: if, for example, only 2 special education students at a school took the state test, NH DOE does not release how many of them scored at each level.
   - citation: NH DOE, "Data Use & Student Privacy," January 2016, pp. 18-19
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/student-privacy.pdf
   - as_of: Wayback snapshot 2025-08-31
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
8. On this site, a district shown only as "<10%" or ">90%" proficient is left out of rankings, and a student group too small to report is left out of the group table.
   - citation: NH Education Facts dataset notes (education-backend, ask/data_tools.py: rank_districts note and test_scores group note)
   - source: https://nhfacts.com
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: the site's own behavior, from the code read this session

## measure-student-growth-percentile: Student Growth Percentile (SGP)

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: What the "Mean SGP" growth measure is and how the state uses it.

1. NH DOE describes its growth measure as showing "how students are improving annually in comparison to students with similar achievement histories."
   - citation: NH DOE, "Federal Accountability System in New Hampshire" fact sheet, p. 4
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/ta_federal-accountability-system-in-new-hampshire.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
2. New Hampshire uses the Student Growth Percentile (SGP) model (Betebenner, 2009) for its growth indicator in elementary and middle schools, and evaluates each school on its mean SGP, called the MGP.
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised February 1, 2023), section A.4.iv.b
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. NH's state plan describes the growth question behind English learners' SGPs on the ACCESS test as how much a student has grown "in comparison to English learner peers with similar histories of prior achievement."
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised February 1, 2023), section A.4.iv.d (Progress in Achieving English Language Proficiency)
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. For federal accountability, NH DOE scores a school's growth at Level 1 below 40.01, Level 2 from 40.01 to below 50, Level 3 from 50 to below 61 and Level 4 from 61 to 99.99.
   - citation: NH DOE, "Federal Accountability System in New Hampshire" fact sheet, p. 6
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/sonh/ta_federal-accountability-system-in-new-hampshire.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
5. NH DOE's test results file reports "Mean SGP" for each school, grade and group; it shows "Not applicable" where no growth is calculated (for example, all grades combined at a high school) and "* n < 11" for small groups.
   - citation: NH DOE, 2025-26 Disaggregated Data File (Regular Denominator)
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt326/files/inline-documents/2026/publicdisaggregateddata2026_csvformat.csv
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08

## measure-state-proficiency-goals: The State's Proficiency Targets and Long-Term Goals

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: New Hampshire's yearly targets and long-term goals for the share of students proficient, graduation and English learners' progress (the goal lines on the site's charts).

1. New Hampshire's ESSA state plan sets statewide targets for the percent of students proficient (Level 3 or 4) in reading and math; after a two-year COVID-19 shift, the targets run from a 2018 baseline of 58.00% in reading and 48.00% in math to long-term goals of 70.61% in reading and 54.21% in math in 2027.
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised February 1, 2023), Table 1, pp. 20-21
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
2. The yearly statewide targets in NH's state plan are: 2019, 2020 and 2021, 60.45% reading and 49.14% math; 2022, 62.54% and 50.14%; 2023, 64.40% and 51.05%; 2024, 66.11% and 51.90%; 2025, 67.69% and 52.70%; 2026, 69.19% and 53.47%; 2027, 70.61% and 54.21%.
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised February 1, 2023), Table 1
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: matches education-backend assets/state_targets.csv (rounded to one decimal)
3. NH's state plan says the goals assume average yearly gains of 1.7 percentage points in English language arts and 0.85 in math, which it calls stretch goals above the historical averages of about 1.4 and 0.375.
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised February 1, 2023), section A.4.iii.a, pp. 19-20
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. The two-year shift of New Hampshire's achievement and English language goals came from a COVID-19 addendum to the state plan approved by the U.S. Department of Education on July 25, 2022.
   - citation: NH ESSA Consolidated State Plan (revised February 1, 2023), "2022 UPDATE," p. 20
   - source: https://www.ed.gov/sites/ed/files/2023/12/nh-state-plan-2023.pdf

   - source: https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/7801
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08

## measure-truancy-vs-chronic-absence: Truancy vs. Chronic Absence

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: The difference between "habitually truant" (the site's measure) and "chronically absent."

1. Under New Hampshire law, truancy is an unexcused absence from school or class, and 10 half-days of unexcused absence in a school year is habitual truancy; each school district defines what a "half day of absence" is.
   - citation: RSA 189:35-a, I-III
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/189/189-35-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
2. NH DOE's School Safety Data Collection asks each school for the number of students habitually truant, "defined as ten half days of unexcused absence during a school year"; this is the truancy count on this site.
   - citation: NH DOE, "2024-2025 NH School Safety Data Collection," Section 6: Truancy (dated July 24, 2026)
   - source: https://www.education.nh.gov/who-we-are/division-of-educator-and-analytic-resources/bureau-of-education-statistics
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: raw/terms/doe/iplatform/safety-2025.xlsx
3. NCES describes chronically absent students as those who missed at least 10 percent of school days, about 18 days in a year, for any reason.
   - citation: NCES, "Chronic Absenteeism" (Supporting Recovery with Evidence-Based Practices)
   - source: https://nces.ed.gov/use-work/supporting-recovery-with-evidence-based-practices/chronic-absenteeism
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. New Hampshire public schools report every student's absences, excused and unexcused, plus in-school and out-of-school suspensions, to NH DOE; NH DOE uses this data to calculate attendance rates and chronic absenteeism for each district and school.
   - citation: NH DOE help desk wiki, "RESOURCES: Student Absence" (updated November 6, 2025); NH DOE 24/25 End of Year Training, Part 3
   - source: https://nhdoepm.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/TRHP/pages/2965536790/RESOURCES+Student+Absence
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
5. So a student can be chronically absent without being truant (for example, many excused absences), and a student can be habitually truant (10 unexcused half-days) without missing 10% of the year.
   - citation: derived from RSA 189:35-a, II and the NCES definition above
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/189/189-35-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

# Part 2. How New Hampshire schools are run

## gov-school-district: School Districts

- kind: term
- question_type: governance_money
- description: What a school district is in New Hampshire and what it can do.

1. Under New Hampshire law, each town is a single school district, except districts organized under special acts of the legislature; in unincorporated places the county is the district.
   - citation: RSA 194:1
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194/194-1.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
2. A New Hampshire school district is a corporation that can sue and be sued, hold and dispose of property for its schools, make contracts, and raise money for school buildings, land, insurance, employee benefits, transportation and other listed purposes.
   - citation: RSA 194:2 and 194:3
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194/194-3.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
3. New Hampshire law requires the school board to provide elementary and secondary education at district expense to all pupils who live in the district until they earn a high school diploma or reach age 21 (21 inclusive for a child with a disability).
   - citation: RSA 189:1-a, I
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/189/189-1-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. For adequacy aid, "school district" includes cooperative school districts.
   - citation: RSA 198:38, VIII
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-38.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - citation: RSA 194:21, I-II
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194/194-21.htm

   - review_by: 2027-08-15

## gov-sau: School Administrative Units (SAUs)

- kind: term
- question_type: governance_money
- description: What an SAU is and what it does for its member districts.

1. A school administrative unit (SAU) is a corporation that provides administrative services to one or more school districts; an SAU may hold property for administration and instruction but may not buy land, build or buy buildings, or borrow to buy real estate.

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

Every New Hampshire SAU or single-district SAU must provide superintendent services, including business administration, payroll, staff recruitment and evaluation, labor contract support, curriculum, compliance with special education and other laws, assessment, transportation, the annual budget and the school calendar.
   - citation: RSA 194-C:4
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194-C/194-C-4.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
3. A New Hampshire school district is not required to have a superintendent; the SAU may assign superintendent services to one or more full- or part-time administrators or contract for them.
   - citation: RSA 194-C:5, II(a)

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. The SAU board sets the salaries of all SAU staff, splits their cost among the member districts, and has the authority to remove superintendents and other administrators.

   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194-C/194-C-5.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
5. A single-district SAU is treated as a single school district, but it must still provide superintendent services, adopt a budget, conduct budget voting under RSA 194-C:9 and hold public hearings.

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

- question_type: governance_money
- description: How districts create, reorganize, merge or withdraw from an SAU, and who votes.

   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194-C/194-C-2.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

## gov-cooperative-district: Cooperative School Districts

- kind: term
- question_type: governance_money

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/195/195-14.htm

## budget-teacher-contracts: Teacher and Staff Contracts: Who Negotiates and Who Approves the Cost

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: How school employee contracts are bargained, why voters vote on the cost items, and what happens if they say no.

1. Under New Hampshire's public employee bargaining law, the school board (the "board of the public employer") and the union certified for the employees must negotiate in good faith, but neither side is required to agree to a proposal or make a concession.

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
3. Only the cost items of a school contract go to the legislative body for approval, at its next annual meeting; voters cannot change a cost item agreed by the board and the union, only accept or reject it.
   - citation: RSA 273-A:3, II(b)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XXIII/273-A/273-A-3.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. If the legislative body rejects the cost items, or acts in a way that would change them, either side may reopen negotiations on the entire agreement.
   - citation: RSA 273-A:3, II(b)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XXIII/273-A/273-A-3.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
5. Where the employer has a city or town council form of government, cost items go to the council within 30 days, and the council must vote to accept or reject them within 30 days.
   - citation: RSA 273-A:3, II(c)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XXIII/273-A/273-A-3.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - citation: RSA 273-A:3, II(a); RSA 273-A:1, III

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

## money-statewide-education-property-tax: The Statewide Education Property Tax

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: How the state education property tax (SWEPT) is set and where the money goes.

1. Each year the Commissioner of Revenue Administration sets the statewide education property tax rate at the level needed to raise $363,000,000 statewide, to the nearest half cent; it applies to all taxable property except utility property taxed under RSA 82 and RSA 83-F.
   - citation: RSA 76:3
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/V/76/76-3.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
2. The Department of Revenue Administration figures each municipality's share by multiplying the uniform education tax rate by the municipality's equalized property value (excluding utility property taxed separately).
   - citation: RSA 76:8, I
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/V/76/76-8.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
3. Each municipality assesses its share of the statewide education tax on local property and pays it to the municipality for the use of its school district or districts.
   - citation: RSA 76:8, II
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/V/76/76-8.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. The statewide education tax raised in a municipality is subtracted from the state's adequacy grant to that municipality.
   - citation: RSA 198:41, I(b)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-41.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
5. The New Hampshire property tax year runs April 1 to March 31.
   - citation: RSA 76:2
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/V/76/76-2.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

## money-adequacy-aid-amounts: Adequacy Aid: Base Amount and Differentiated Aid

   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-40-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. Since May 26, 2026, RSA 198:40-a, III says the total calculated under paragraph II, including all differentiated aid, is "computed toward" the cost of an adequate education as defined in RSA 193-E:2-a; the earlier text said the total "shall be the cost of an adequate education."

   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-40-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
   - note: earlier text in raw/terms/rsa/prior/198-40-a-wayback-2025.html

   - note: the site's EFA data still has a "3rd Grade Reading Aid" type from earlier years

## money-adequacy-grant-calculation: How a Town's Adequacy Grant Is Calculated and Paid

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: The formula that turns the per-pupil amounts into each town's grant, and when the money arrives.

1. NH DOE calculates each municipality's education grant by adding the adequacy amounts for its pupils, subtracting the statewide education tax raised in the municipality, and adding its extraordinary need grant and fiscal capacity disparity aid.
   - citation: RSA 198:41, I(a)-(d)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-41.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
2. Beginning July 1, 2027, for municipalities with total ADMR of 5,000 or more, NH DOE will reduce the grant as needed to limit extraordinary need grants plus fiscal capacity disparity aid to $3,750 per pupil.
   - citation: RSA 198:41, I(e) (2025, 142:3, eff. July 1, 2027)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-41.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
3. NH DOE estimates each municipality's grant by November 15 of the year before the school year, issues a revised estimate for setting tax rates, and makes the final determination by October 1; a final grant may not be less than 95% of the November estimate.
   - citation: RSA 198:41, IV-VI
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-41.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. The adequacy grant is paid from the education trust fund to each municipality's school district or districts in four payments: 20% on September 1, 20% on November 1, 30% on January 1 and 30% on April 1.
   - citation: RSA 198:42, I
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-42.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
5. The money needed for adequacy grants is appropriated automatically from the education trust fund each year, and if the fund falls below zero the state comptroller transfers money from the general fund.
   - citation: RSA 198:42, II
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-42.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
6. Because the statewide education tax is subtracted, a municipality whose education tax raises more than its adequacy amounts receives no adequacy grant; the State has acknowledged in court that since 2011 such communities have been permitted to keep the excess education tax.
   - citation: RSA 198:41, I(b); Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27, ¶2 (the State's admission that "since 2011" communities have been permitted to retain the excess)
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-06/2025027rand.pdf

   - review_by: 2027-11-08

## money-special-education-aid: Special Education (Catastrophic) Aid

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: State help with very high-cost special education students, and the change coming July 1, 2028.

1. Under current New Hampshire law, a district is responsible for a special education student's costs up to 3.5 times the state average expenditure per pupil, plus 20% of costs between 3.5 and 10 times; the state pays 80% of costs between 3.5 and 10 times and all costs above 10 times.
   - citation: RSA 186-C:18, III(b)-(c) (effective until July 1, 2028)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/186-C/186-C-18.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15

   - citation: RSA 186-C:18, III(a)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/186-C/186-C-18.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
3. Starting with fiscal year 2029 (based on 2027-28 costs), aid begins at 2.5 times the state average: districts pay 85% and the state 15% of costs between 2.5 and 3.5 times; districts 20% and the state 80% between 3.5 and 10 times; and districts 10% and the state 90% above 10 times.

   - as_of: 2026-11-08

   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
5. Districts submit their prior-year special education costs by July 31, and the state distributes the aid by January 1.
   - citation: RSA 186-C:18, VI(a)
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/186-C/186-C-18.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08

## money-conval-case: The ConVal School Funding Case (2025)

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: What the New Hampshire Supreme Court decided about the base adequacy amount, and the case's status.

1. In Contoocook Valley School District v. State, 2025 N.H. 29 (Case No. 2024-0121), decided July 1, 2025, the New Hampshire Supreme Court agreed with the trial court that the State had not met its constitutional duty under Part II, Article 83 to determine and fund the cost of a constitutionally adequate education.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶1
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: raw/terms/courts/2025-NH-29-ConVal-v-State.pdf
2. The plaintiffs in the ConVal case were eighteen school districts (Contoocook Valley, Winchester, Mascenic Regional, Monadnock Regional, Fall Mountain, Claremont, Newport, Hillsboro-Deering, Grantham, Oyster River Cooperative, Manchester, Windham, Derry Cooperative, Hill, Mascoma Valley Regional, Nashua, Lebanon and Hopkinton) and two individuals.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, footnote 1
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. The court affirmed the trial court's declaration that RSA 198:40-a, II(a), the $4,100 base adequacy amount, is facially unconstitutional because it does not provide enough funding to cover the cost of the opportunity for an adequate education as defined in RSA 193-E:2-a.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶¶2 and 77
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. The court affirmed the trial court's "conservative minimum threshold" of $7,356.01 per pupil that base adequacy aid must exceed, as guidance for future legislative action.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶¶60 and 67
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
5. The court reversed the trial court's order that the State immediately pay base adequacy aid of $7,356.01 per pupil pending legislative action.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶¶1 and 77
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
6. The trial court built the $7,356.01 figure from per-pupil costs for teachers ($3,981.01), principals, administrative assistants, guidance counselors, library and media specialists, technology coordinators, custodians, nurses, instructional materials, technology, teacher professional development, facilities operation and maintenance ($1,000) and transportation ($750).
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶64
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
7. The court held that base adequacy aid must be enough on its own to provide the opportunity for an adequate education in every district, and that the trial court properly did not count differentiated aid when judging the base amount.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶45 and section on differentiated aid
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
8. The court divided: Justice Bassett and two specially assigned retired superior court justices (Nadeau and Abramson) formed the majority on the unconstitutionality ruling and the threshold; Justices Countway and Donovan joined Justice Bassett in reversing the immediate-payment order but otherwise dissented.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶60 and closing notation
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
9. The court wrote that "it is now incumbent upon the legislative and executive branches to remedy the constitutional deficiency," and urged them to act expeditiously.
   - citation: Contoocook Valley Sch. Dist. v. State, 2025 N.H. 29, ¶78
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/2025029conval.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
10. On July 24, 2025, the New Hampshire Supreme Court denied the State's motion for reconsideration and re-argument in the ConVal case.
   - citation: N.H. Supreme Court order, Case No. 2024-0121, July 24, 2025
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-07/07-24-2025-order-denying-reconsideration.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
11. As published on gc.nh.gov when checked, RSA 198:40-a, II(a) still sets base adequacy at $4,100 per pupil for the biennium beginning July 1, 2023, subject to the 2% yearly adjustment that began July 1, 2026.
   - citation: RSA 198:40-a, I-II; RSA 198:40-d
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/198/198-40-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
   - note: a statement about the statute text only; no court or state source was found saying whether the legislature has addressed the ruling

## money-rand-case: The Rand Statewide Education Tax Case (2025) and Its Appeal

- kind: how_it_works
- question_type: governance_money
- description: What the New Hampshire Supreme Court decided about the statewide education property tax, and the later trial court ruling now on appeal.

1. In Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27 (Case No. 2024-0138), decided June 10, 2025, the New Hampshire Supreme Court held that letting communities keep statewide education property tax (SWEPT) revenue that exceeds the cost of an adequate education does not violate Part II, Article 5 of the State Constitution; it is an exercise of the legislature's spending power.
   - citation: Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27, ¶¶1 and 17
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-06/2025027rand.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: raw/terms/courts/2025-NH-27-Rand-v-State.pdf
2. The court held the SWEPT constitutional on that issue because it is "administered in a manner that is equal in valuation and uniform in rate throughout the State," reversed the trial court, and vacated the trial court's injunction on the excess-SWEPT issue.
   - citation: Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27, ¶17
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-06/2025027rand.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
3. The court also held that the Department of Revenue Administration's practice of setting negative local education tax rates in certain unincorporated places, which nearly or completely offset the SWEPT there, violates Part II, Article 5.
   - citation: Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27, ¶¶21-23
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-06/2025027rand.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
4. As an example, the court noted that in 2021 the local education tax rate for Hale's Location was set at negative $1.84 per thousand, which with its SWEPT rate of $1.85 gave an effective rate of $0.01 per thousand.
   - citation: Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27, ¶22
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-06/2025027rand.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
5. The court held that "municipality" in RSA 76:8 includes unincorporated places, so they are not exempt from the SWEPT; it vacated the trial court's injunction on negative rates as unnecessary, leaving the fix to the other branches, and remanded.
   - citation: Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27, ¶¶21 and 24
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-06/2025027rand.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
6. Chief Justice MacDonald wrote the Rand opinion; Justices Donovan and Countway concurred, and Justice Bassett concurred on negative tax rates but dissented on the excess-SWEPT issue.
   - citation: Rand v. State, 2025 N.H. 27, opinion and closing notation
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-06/2025027rand.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
7. On August 18, 2025, the Rockingham Superior Court (Ruoff, J.) ruled on the Rand plaintiffs' remaining claim, declaring RSA 198:40-a, II unconstitutional because total adequacy funding and special education differentiated aid are insufficient, and declaring that the resulting reliance on local school taxes violates Part II, Article 5.
   - citation: Rand v. State, No. 215-2022-CV-00167, Order on the Merits (Rockingham Super. Ct. Aug. 18, 2025), pp. 49-50
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-08/081825%20215-2022-CV-00167%20Order%20on%20Merits%20157.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
8. In the same August 18, 2025 order, the Superior Court denied the claim that aid for students living in poverty is insufficient, and denied injunctive relief without prejudice.
   - citation: Rand v. State, No. 215-2022-CV-00167, Order on the Merits, pp. 50-52
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2025-08/081825%20215-2022-CV-00167%20Order%20on%20Merits%20157.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
9. The Superior Court denied the State's motion for reconsideration on January 26, 2026, and the State filed a notice of appeal to the New Hampshire Supreme Court on February 24, 2026.
   - citation: Rand v. State, No. 215-2022-CV-00167, Order on the State's Motion for Reconsideration (Jan. 26, 2026); Rule 7 Notice of Mandatory Appeal (filed Feb. 24, 2026)
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2026-01/012626-215-2022-cv-00167-court-order-denied-163.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
10. The New Hampshire Supreme Court accepted the State's appeal as Case No. 2026-0150 on April 2, 2026, and on June 2, 2026 ordered the trial record transferred by June 22, 2026; these orders were the latest Supreme Court documents on the court's Rand case page when checked.
   - citation: N.H. Supreme Court orders in Case No. 2026-0150 (April 2, 2026 and June 2, 2026)
   - source: https://www.courts.nh.gov/sites/g/files/ehbemt471/files/documents/2026-08/6.9.26-supreme-court-order.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: case page https://www.courts.nh.gov/media/requested-cases/civil/steven-rand-et-al-v-state-new-hampshire checked 2026-11-08 in a browser

## glossary-rules-one-liners: Short Definitions of Rules Terms

- kind: term
- question_type: meaning
- description: One-line definitions, with citations, for terms whose rules and rights are covered in facts-rules-and-rights.md.

1. A "chartered public school" in New Hampshire is an open enrollment public school, operated independent of any school board and managed by a board of trustees, as a nonprofit secular organization under a charter granted by the State Board of Education.
   - citation: RSA 194-B:1, IV
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194-B/194-B-1.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
2. A "school tuition program" is a private school approved and contracted by a school board to give students the opportunity for an adequate education, which a district without a public school at a child's grade may pay tuition to.
   - citation: RSA 193:3, VI-VII
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/193/193-3.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
3. An Education Freedom Account (EFA) is a state-funded account that parents may use only for listed educational expenses, such as private school tuition, tutoring, curriculum, therapies and fees.
   - citation: RSA 194-F:2, II-III
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/194-F/194-F-2.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
4. "Habitual truancy" in New Hampshire is 10 half-days of unexcused absence in a school year.
   - citation: RSA 189:35-a, II
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/189/189-35-a.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
5. A "child with a disability" under New Hampshire special education law is a person aged 3 to 21 inclusive who has been identified and evaluated by the school district as having a listed disability and needing special education.
   - citation: RSA 186-C:2, I
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/186-C/186-C-2.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
6. "Substantiated" bullying incidents are the ones New Hampshire districts and chartered public schools must report to NH DOE each year.
   - citation: RSA 193-F:6, I
   - source: https://gc.nh.gov/rsa/html/XV/193-F/193-F-6.htm
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-08-15
