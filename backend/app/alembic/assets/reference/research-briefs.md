# Research Briefs: What the Research Says

Strategy type 7. Short, neutral summaries of strong studies on recurring "does it work?" questions, so the Ask can answer them from reviewed sources instead of from memory. Each brief is a topic of kind `research` with a `summary` line, followed by one finding per fact.

## Summary

- **Status (2026-11-08):** 10 briefs, 95 facts. They draw on 84 studies, reviews and federal guides, plus 5 New Hampshire documents on what is reported about EFA students: the law, the Department of Education's EFA report, the program administrator's dashboard, the legislative oversight committee's 2025 draft report and a 2026 bill. Every finding comes from a document opened this session. For 18 sources only the abstract could be read, and each of those says so in its `note`.
- **Briefs:**
  1. School spending (9 facts)
  2. Private school choice and EFAs (16)
  3. Full-day kindergarten (9)
  4. Class size (9)
  5. District and school consolidation (8)
  6. Career and technical education (10)
  7. Dual enrollment (9)
  8. Helping young children read at home (9)
  9. Chronic absence (8)
  10. School phone restrictions (8)
- **Where the evidence is thin:**
  - **Education savings accounts:** no randomized or comparison-group study of a program like New Hampshire's EFAs was found. Nearly all rigorous research is on voucher and tax-credit scholarship programs, mostly for lower-income families. New Hampshire has published no aggregate results for EFA students.
  - **Phone restrictions:** there are no randomized trials, and results are mixed.
  - **Full-day kindergarten:** there is one lottery study, and it measured only the end of kindergarten. Long-run results disagree.
  - **Career and technical education (CTE):** earnings were measured in only two causal studies. No causal study covers part-day regional centers, which is how New Hampshire mainly delivers CTE.
  - **Consolidation:** 6 of 8 sources are abstract only, and there is no New Hampshire study.
  - **Dual enrollment:** Running Start and eStart have never been evaluated.
  - **Reading at home:** the federal home tips have not been tested with parents at home.
- **New Hampshire evidence:** no cause-and-effect study of New Hampshire was found for any topic. The New Hampshire items are descriptive: a 2020 school funding cost model, a 2021 study of who gets access to CTE, a 2017 table of dual enrollment outcomes by state, and the EFA reporting documents.
- **Versions:** several numbers come from working-paper versions, and the notes say which. One correction matters for the Ask's existing reference facts: the published Jackson and Mackevicius (2024) meta-analysis reports 0.0316 standard deviations and 2.8 percentage points. Test #3's reference facts cite that paper's 2021 working-paper figures (0.0352 SD; 1.92 and 2.65 percentage points).

## Terms used in the briefs

- **Standard deviation (SD):** a measure of how spread out scores are. Many studies report effects as a share of one SD (for example, "0.10 standard deviations"). Some briefs instead give percentile points, percentage points or percent changes, as each source states them.
- **Randomized or lottery study:** students or schools are assigned by chance, so the groups are alike except for the program.
- **Natural experiment or cutoff study:** compares students on either side of a rule, a close vote or an admissions cutoff.
- **Matched comparison:** compares participants with similar students who did not take part. It cannot rule out unmeasured differences such as motivation.
- **Meta-analysis:** a study that combines the results of many studies.

## research-school-spending: Does More School Spending Improve Results?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What careful U.S. studies find about whether more money for public schools changes test scores, graduation, college-going and adult earnings.
- summary: Recent U.S. studies designed to separate the effect of money from the effect of students' backgrounds mostly find that sustained spending increases improve outcomes on average, by modest amounts: a 2024 review of these studies estimated that $1,000 more per student for four years raised test scores by 0.0316 standard deviations and college-going by 2.8 percentage points, with smaller effects for economically advantaged students. Effects vary widely from place to place, and researchers disagree about how far the averages generalize; a 2023 review of many of the same studies stressed that results range from negative to large and that how money is used is key to results. Studies disagree on buildings: some find little effect of construction and renovation spending, while the 2024 review found effects similar to other spending. No cause-and-effect study of New Hampshire districts was found; a 2020 New Hampshire cost model is descriptive.

1. A 2024 meta-analysis of U.S. studies of K-12 public school spending found that, on average, a policy that raised spending by $1,000 per student for four years improved test scores by 0.0316 standard deviations and raised college-going by 2.8 percentage points. It found smaller effects for economically advantaged students, similar effects for capital (building) and non-capital spending, similar effects across baseline spending levels and geography, and little sign of bias from confounding or selective publication.
   - citation: Jackson, C. K., & Mackevicius, C. L. (2024). What Impacts Can We Expect from School Spending Policy? Evidence from Evaluations in the United States. American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 16(1), 412–446.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20220279
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Published abstract only (AEA article page); the published article is paywalled. Its results section was read in the 2021 working-paper version (NBER WP 28517, 31 studies; raw/research/nber-w28517-jackson-mackevicius.pdf), whose figures differ: 0.0352 SD for test scores, 1.92 points for graduation and 2.65 points for college-going, with capital spending effects "about half" those of other spending. Test #3's reference facts cite the working-paper figures.

2. In the 2021 working-paper version of the Jackson and Mackevicius meta-analysis (31 "credibly causal" U.S. studies), the authors found that most of the variation between studies (76 to 88 percent) reflects real differences between settings. A $1,000-per-student increase sustained for four years in a new setting would therefore be expected to change test scores by between −0.007 and 0.078 standard deviations 90 percent of the time, and to raise them more than 91 percent of the time. The 90 percent ranges were −0.2 to 4.1 percentage points for high school graduation and −0.3 to 5.65 percentage points for college-going.
   - citation: Jackson, C. K., & Mackevicius, C. (2021). The Distribution of School Spending Impacts. NBER Working Paper 28517 (February 2021, revised July 2021).
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w28517
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract and pp. 3–4 (PDF p. 7). Not peer reviewed in this version; the published 2024 version (fact 1) uses a larger study set and different headline figures, and its prediction ranges were not read. raw/research/nber-w28517-jackson-mackevicius.pdf

3. A study of court-ordered school finance reforms followed a national sample of children born from 1955 to 1985 into adulthood (through 2011). It found that a 10 percent increase in per-pupil spending in each of a child's 12 school years led to 0.27 more completed years of education, 7.25 percent higher adult wages and a 3.67 percentage-point lower yearly chance of adult poverty. For children from low-income families the effects were larger: 0.43 more years of education, 9.5 percent higher earnings and a 6.8 percentage-point lower yearly chance of adult poverty. The effects for children from more affluent families were small.
   - citation: Jackson, C. K., Johnson, R. C., & Persico, C. (2016). The Effects of School Spending on Educational and Economic Outcomes: Evidence from School Finance Reforms. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 131(1), 157–218.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjv036
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read NBER Working Paper 20847 (January 2015): abstract, pp. 3–4 and the data section. The published version was not opened, so its numbers may differ. The data are from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics: 9,035 low-income and 6,318 non-poor children from 1,409 school districts. The authors report that the spending increases came with fewer students per teacher, higher teacher salaries and longer school years. raw/research/nber-w20847-jackson-johnson-persico.pdf

4. A study of school finance reforms adopted by U.S. states after 1990 used National Assessment of Educational Progress (NAEP) scores for 4th and 8th graders from 1990 to 2011. It found that reforms raised spending in low-income districts by about $1,200 per pupil per year, or about $700 relative to high-income districts. Ten years after a reform, the relative achievement of students in low-income districts had risen by roughly 0.1 standard deviation, about one-fifth of the starting gap between high- and low-income districts. The authors put the implied effect at 0.12 to 0.24 standard deviations per $1,000 per pupil in annual spending. They found no discernible effect on the overall achievement gap between high- and low-income students, because low-income students are not highly concentrated in low-income districts.
   - citation: Lafortune, J., Rothstein, J., & Schanzenbach, D. W. (2018). School Finance Reform and the Distribution of Student Achievement. American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 10(2), 1–26.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20160567
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read NBER Working Paper 22011 (February 2016, revised July 2016): abstract and introduction (pp. 2–6). The published version was not opened. The study compares 26 states that reformed with 23 that did not. Its per-$1,000 figure is for sustained annual spending, a different measure from fact 1's four-year measure, so the two are not directly comparable. raw/research/spend-nber-w22011-lafortune-rothstein-schanzenbach.pdf

5. A 2023 review of school spending research, written for the Handbook of the Economics of Education, converted 16 U.S. studies with strong research designs to a common measure. The median effect of a 10 percent spending increase was 0.07 standard deviations in test scores, but the estimates ranged from −0.244 to +0.543 standard deviations. Most estimates were positive, but only nine were statistically different from zero at the 5 percent level. The authors concluded that how resources are used is key to results, and that research has not identified when added money is likely to be used well. They also found that studies of specific uses (capital spending, smaller classes, salary incentives for teachers) do not clearly support any one of them in the United States.
   - citation: Handel, D. V., & Hanushek, E. A. (2023). U.S. School Finance: Resources and Outcomes. In E. A. Hanushek, S. Machin & L. Woessmann (Eds.), Handbook of the Economics of Education, Vol. 7. North Holland. (NBER Working Paper 30769, December 2022, revised February 2023.)
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w30769
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract and Conclusions (pp. 44–45). The paper discloses that Hanushek consults and testifies on school finance for both plaintiffs and defendants. It uses a 10-percent measure rather than a $1,000 measure, so its numbers are not directly comparable with facts 1–2. In footnote 45 the authors report a pooled random-effects mean of 0.066, which they say "has little meaning" given how varied the studies are. raw/research/spend-nber-w30769-handel-hanushek.pdf

6. In Wisconsin, districts must win a local referendum to exceed state revenue limits, and they vote separately on operating money and on building bonds. A study compared districts where these referenda narrowly passed or failed (1996-97 to 2014-15). Narrowly passing an operating referendum raised per-pupil spending by about 5 percent, mostly for instruction. It led to a 25 percent drop in the district's dropout rate, district test scores about 30 percent of a (district-level) standard deviation higher, and a 15 percent rise in graduates going on to postsecondary education. Narrowly passing a building bond raised capital spending, mostly to repair, maintain or upgrade buildings, but was not associated with improved student outcomes.
   - citation: Baron, E. J. (2022). School Spending and Student Outcomes: Evidence from Revenue Limit Elections in Wisconsin. American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 14(1), 1–39.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20200226
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Figures from the October 2019 working paper (AEA conference copy: https://www.aeaweb.org/conference/2020/preliminary/paper/NKFEh2BY), abstract and pp. 4–5. The published version was not opened, so its figures may differ; a later working-paper abstract gives the same direction (substantial positive effects of operating spending; capital spending "little impact"). The test-score effect is in district-level standard-deviation units, so it is not comparable with the student-level figures in facts 1–5. raw/research/spend-baron-wisconsin-wp2019.pdf

7. A study of nearly 1,400 local school construction and renovation bond campaigns in Texas over a 14-year period compared districts where bonds narrowly passed with districts where they narrowly failed. It found little evidence that these capital campaigns improved student achievement. For students attending schools that received major renovations, the estimated effects were "very precise zero estimates."
   - citation: Martorell, P., Stange, K., & McFarlin, I. (2016). Investing in schools: Capital spending, facility conditions, and student achievement. Journal of Public Economics.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2016.05.002
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read NBER Working Paper 21515 (September 2015): abstract and introduction. The published version was not opened (NBER lists it, without volume or pages). On buildings this contrasts with the published 2024 meta-analysis (fact 1), which found capital and other spending had similar effects. raw/research/spend-nber-w21515-martorell-stange-mcfarlin.pdf

8. A study of federal pandemic relief money spent in the 2022-23 school year by more than 5,000 districts in 29 states found that each $1,000 per student spent was associated with test-score gains of 0.0086 standard deviations in math and 0.0049 in reading. The authors say this is consistent with pre-pandemic research. Relief money that districts had not yet spent showed no relationship with achievement growth. A second comparison, of high-poverty districts that got large or small amounts of relief, found effects of roughly 0.02 standard deviations per $1,000, though with large confidence intervals.
   - citation: Dewey, D. C., Fahle, E. M., Kane, T. J., Reardon, S. F., & Staiger, D. O. (2024). Federal Pandemic Relief and Academic Recovery. NBER Working Paper 32897 (September 2024).
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w32897
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract and pp. 2–4. Not peer reviewed. This measures one year of spending, not a four-year increase. The authors compare their result with a figure of .0079 SD per $1,000 that they attribute to Jackson and Mackevicius (2024). raw/research/spend-nber-w32897-dewey-esser.pdf

9. In a 2020 study of New Hampshire school districts for the state's Commission to Study School Funding, the American Institutes for Research reported that districts with higher shares of economically disadvantaged students (measured by free or reduced-price lunch eligibility), students in special education and English learners performed worse on average. Its cost model estimated that higher-need districts and small districts require more spending per student to reach a common level of student outcomes. The cost model is a statistical estimate of the spending associated with given outcomes across New Hampshire districts, using 2018–19 data. ## research-private-school-choice: Do Private School Choice Programs, Such as Education Freedom Accounts, Change Students' Results?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What careful studies find about vouchers, tax-credit scholarships and education savings accounts and students' test scores, graduation and college-going, and what New Hampshire collects and publishes about Education Freedom Account students' results.
- summary: Most rigorous studies of private school choice cover voucher and tax-credit scholarship programs for lower-income students, and results differ by program and outcome: lottery studies of Louisiana's and Washington, D.C.'s programs in the 2010s found lower math scores in the first one or two years that shrank or disappeared in several later years, while a 2021 review of 21 lottery studies worldwide found moderate evidence of positive effects overall with wide variation. Effects on attainment have been positive in some programs (high school graduation in D.C.'s earlier program; college enrollment in Florida and Milwaukee in matched comparisons) and not detectable in others (college entry in Louisiana and in D.C.'s lottery). This review found no rigorous study of an education savings account program, the type of program New Hampshire's Education Freedom Accounts (EFAs) are, which can pay for private school, tutoring, online programs, materials and therapies. New Hampshire law requires an annual record of each EFA student's educational attainment, but neither the state Department of Education nor the program administrator has published aggregate results, so there is no public data on how EFA students are doing.

1. A meta-analysis of all randomized (lottery) studies of the math and reading effects of private school vouchers worldwide screened 9,443 potential studies and included 21, covering 11 voucher programs. It found "moderate evidence of positive achievement impacts" of vouchers, with substantial variation in effects across programs and across the years after students received vouchers.
   - citation: Shakeel, M. D., Anderson, K. P., & Wolf, P. J. (2021). The Participant Effects of Private School Vouchers around the Globe: A Meta-Analytic and Systematic Review. School Effectiveness and School Improvement.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1080/09243453.2021.1906283
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract only, via the ERIC record EJ1324364. The journal is paywalled, and the authors' 2016 working paper at scholarworks.uark.edu was blocked by a bot check in both curl and Chrome. Earlier versions (2016 working paper, 19 studies; 2016 SREE abstract, ERIC ED567044) report different subgroup patterns (for example, larger effects for reading than math, and outside the U.S.), so only the published abstract is used. The authors are at the University of Arkansas, whose Department of Education Reform also studies school choice programs.

2. A lottery-based study of the Louisiana Scholarship Program looked at students in grades 3 to 8 who applied in fall 2012, the first year after the voucher program for low-income students expanded statewide. Using a voucher to attend a participating private school lowered math scores by 0.4 standard deviations one year later and raised the chance of a failing math score by 50 percent. Effects on reading, science and social studies were also negative and large. The negative math effects were concentrated in participating schools with lower tuition, and the authors suggest the program may have drawn lower-quality private schools.
   - citation: Abdulkadiroğlu, A., Pathak, P. A., & Walters, C. R. (2018). Free to Choose: Can School Choice Reduce Student Achievement? American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 10(1), 175–206.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1257/app.20160634
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read NBER Working Paper 21839 (December 2015): abstract and pp. 2–3 and 12–13. The precise math estimate is 0.41σ (p. 12). The published version was not opened. These are first-year effects only; see facts 3–4 for later years. raw/research/efa-nber-w21839-abdulkadiroglu-pathak-walters.pdf

3. A lottery-based study of nearly 10,000 eligible applicants to the Louisiana Scholarship Program for 2012-13 found large negative effects in the first year, especially in math, and some improvement in the second and third years. By year 3, the effect on English language arts was positive and the effect on math negative, but neither was statistically significant. The authors note that this partly reflects shrinking sample sizes and students returning to public schools. Students with lower starting English language arts scores gained in that subject, while students who applied in the early elementary grades had large losses in math.
   - citation: Mills, J. N., & Wolf, P. J. (2017). The Effects of the Louisiana Scholarship Program on Student Achievement after Three Years (Louisiana Scholarship Program Evaluation Report #7, updated July 2017). School Choice Demonstration Project, University of Arkansas, and Education Research Alliance for New Orleans, Tulane University.
   - source: https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED590218.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract and summary of findings (pp. 2–4). Technical report, not peer reviewed. The same team's later peer-reviewed article covering four years (fact 4) reports that the negative effects persisted, so the two versions should be read together. raw/research/efa-mills-wolf-2017-louisiana-3yr.pdf

4. A peer-reviewed study of the Louisiana Scholarship Program, using the program's admission lotteries, followed students for four years after the 2012 statewide expansion. On state tests in math, English language arts and science, it found "substantial negative achievement impacts, especially in math, that diminish after the first year but persist after four years." The study found no statistically significant difference in the likelihood of entering college between students who used scholarships and comparison students.
   - citation: Erickson, H. H., Mills, J. N., & Wolf, P. J. (2021). The Effects of the Louisiana Scholarship Program on Student Achievement and College Entrance. Journal of Research on Educational Effectiveness.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1080/19345747.2021.1938311
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract only, via the ERIC record EJ1314473. The journal is paywalled, and the 2019 four-year working paper on SSRN (abstract_id 3376230) was blocked by a bot check, so the year-by-year effect sizes were not read. The finding differs from the authors' three-year report (fact 3).

5. A federally mandated randomized evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program studied 1,771 eligible applicants who entered lotteries in 2012, 2013 and 2014. Most were entering grades K–5 and scored around the 32nd to 34th national percentile when they applied. Three years after applying, there were no statistically significant effects on reading or math. Students who used a scholarship scored 0.2 percentile points higher in math and 2.1 percentile points lower in reading than the control group. The negative math effects found in the first two years were not found in the third year, and the program had no effect on reading in any of the three years.
   - citation: Webber, A., Rui, N., Garrison-Mogren, R., Olsen, R. B., & Gutmann, B. (2019). Evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program: Impacts Three Years After Students Applied (NCEE 2019-4006).    - source: https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20194006/pdf/20194006.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: pp. 2–4 and 10. In the third year 69 percent of students completed reading tests and 68 percent math tests. The study used weights for nonresponse and found no link between baseline scores and testing. The test used was the TerraNova (nationally normed). raw/research/efa-ies-ncee-2019-4006-dc-osp-3yr.pdf

6. The 2019 federal evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program summarized recent studies of state voucher programs in Louisiana, Indiana and Ohio. All of them indicated that students offered or using vouchers tended to score worse in math after one and two years than similar students not using vouchers, and reading results were mixed. A few of the studies found that the negative effects lessened over time, so that after three and four years students no longer performed worse than similar peers. The evaluation said that what happens five or more years after students receive vouchers is an open question.
   - citation: Webber, A., Rui, N., Garrison-Mogren, R., Olsen, R. B., & Gutmann, B. (2019). Evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program: Impacts Three Years After Students Applied (NCEE 2019-4006), Section 4, "Implications of This Final Report's Results."    - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: pp. 14–15. The report cites Abdulkadiroglu, Pathak & Walters (2016), Mills & Wolf (2017b), Waddington & Berends (2017) and Figlio & Karbownik (2016). The Indiana and Ohio studies themselves were not opened; this fact rests on the federal report's summary of them.

7. The earlier federal evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program followed about 2,300 applicants who entered lotteries mainly in spring 2004 and spring 2005. It found no conclusive evidence of an effect on reading or math scores after at least four years. Students offered a scholarship were more likely to graduate from high school: the graduation rate, based on parents' reports, was 82 percent for students offered a scholarship and 70 percent for those not offered one, a 12 percentage-point difference.
   - citation: Wolf, P., Gutmann, B., Puma, M., Kisida, B., Rizzo, L., Eissa, N., & Carr, M. (2010). Evaluation of the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program: Final Report (NCEE 2010-4018).    - source: https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/pubs/20104018/pdf/20104018.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Executive summary (PDF pp. 16–17). Graduation was measured by parent report, not school records (the report notes this limitation). For students from schools designated "in need of improvement," the rates were 79 percent and 66 percent. raw/research/efa-ies-ncee-2010-4018-dc-osp-final.pdf

8. An Urban Institute report studied two means-tested programs: Florida's Tax Credit Scholarship and Milwaukee's Parental Choice Program. Each compared participating students with similar students who stayed in public schools (a matched comparison, not a lottery). Participants were more likely to enroll in and graduate from college. In Florida, college enrollment was 6 to 10 percentage points higher and bachelor's degree attainment 1 or 2 points higher. In Milwaukee, enrollment was 4 to 6 points and attainment 1 to 3 points higher. Even so, only 10 to 12 percent of Florida participants completed a bachelor's degree.
   - citation: Chingos, M. M., Kuehn, D., Monarrez, T., Wolf, P. J., Witte, J. F., & Kisida, B. (2019). The Effects of Means-Tested Private School Choice Programs on College Enrollment and Graduation. Urban Institute (July 2019).
   - source: https://www.urban.org/sites/default/files/publication/100665/the_effects_of_means-tested_private_school_choice_programs_on_college_enrollment_and_graduation_2.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Conclusion (pp. 21–22) and Florida results (pp. 4–5). Florida's comparison used 16,111 participants, each matched to up to five similar non-participants. The authors say unmeasured differences, such as parental engagement, could bias the results (p. 4). Not peer reviewed. A NEPC review by T. J. Brewer (October 2019) argues the conclusions are overstated; it was found but not opened. raw/research/efa-urban-2019-chingos-college-enrollment.pdf

9. In the same Urban Institute report, the only lottery-based results came from the DC Opportunity Scholarship Program, and they showed no statistically detectable difference in college enrollment. Within two years of expected high school graduation, 43 percent of students offered a scholarship had enrolled in college, compared with 46 percent of applicants who lost the lottery, a difference that was not statistically significant. The authors note that the DC sample was smaller, and that DC students who lost the lottery had much more access to public charter schools than comparison students in Florida and Milwaukee.

   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: pp. 18–19 and Conclusion (p. 21). These are intent-to-treat estimates (the effect of being offered a scholarship); 70 percent of lottery winners used one for at least a year. raw/research/efa-urban-2019-chingos-college-enrollment.pdf

10. A study of Florida's Tax Credit Scholarship program as it grew to more than 100,000 students looked at students who stayed in public schools. Using student records merged with birth records, it found that the program's expansion brought modestly larger gains to public schools that faced more nearby private-school competition before the program began. In its preferred measure, a 10 percent increase in program participation raised math scores by 0.3 percent of a standard deviation and reading scores by more than 0.7 percent, in schools with above-median versus below-median competitive pressure, and lowered suspensions and absences. The effects were largest for lower-income students but were also positive for more affluent students.
   - citation: Figlio, D. N., Hart, C. M. D., & Karbownik, K. (2020). Effects of Scaling Up Private School Choice Programs on Public School Students. NBER Working Paper 26758 (February 2020).

## research-full-day-kindergarten: Does Full-Day Kindergarten Make a Difference?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What strong studies find about full-day versus half-day kindergarten, and how long any difference lasts.
- summary: Studies consistently find that children in full-day kindergarten end the kindergarten year with stronger reading and math skills than children in half-day programs. A 2010 meta-analysis put the gap at about a quarter of a standard deviation, and the only randomized (lottery) study, from Indiana, found 0.31 standard deviations in literacy. Most studies that follow children longer find the advantage shrinks or disappears by about third grade, though some find lasting gains for certain groups, such as in reading or for low-income girls in math. Most of the evidence does not come from randomized experiments, findings on behavior and attitudes toward school are mixed, and little is known about long-run outcomes such as graduation or earnings.

1. A 2010 meta-analysis of studies comparing full-day with half-day kindergarten found that full-day attendance was associated with higher academic achievement, about one quarter of a standard deviation at the end of the kindergarten year. The association disappeared by third grade.
   - citation: Cooper, H., Allen, A. B., Patall, E. A., & Dent, A. L. (2010). Effects of Full-Day Kindergarten on Academic Achievement and Social Development. Review of Educational Research, 80(1), 34–70.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654309359185
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Full text is paywalled (SAGE); the finding is from the abstract only, read via ERIC record EJ879414 (https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ879414). The CDC Community Guide page describes this review as covering 55 studies, with searches through 2009, and no randomized trials.

2. The same 2010 meta-analysis found mixed results for social development. The evidence suggested a small positive association between full-day kindergarten and attendance, and a larger positive association with children's self-confidence and ability to work and play with others. But children in full-day programs may have a less positive attitude toward school and more behavior problems. The authors said future studies allowing strong causal conclusions were needed.
   - citation: Cooper, H., Allen, A. B., Patall, E. A., & Dent, A. L. (2010). Effects of Full-Day Kindergarten on Academic Achievement and Social Development. Review of Educational Research, 80(1), 34–70.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654309359185
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract only (via ERIC); results section not accessible.

3. A 2014 systematic review for the federal Community Preventive Services Task Force drew on the 2010 meta-analysis of U.S. studies. It reported that full-day kindergarten improved academic achievement compared with half-day kindergarten by an average of 0.35 standard deviations (95% confidence interval 0.23 to 0.46), with 0.46 for verbal achievement and 0.24 for math. It also reported that findings on longer-term effects suggested "fade-out" by third grade, and concluded that intensive elementary school education is needed to sustain early benefits.
   - citation: Hahn, R. A., Rammohan, V., Truman, B. I., et al.; Community Preventive Services Task Force (2014). Effects of full-day kindergarten on the long-term health prospects of children in low-income and racial/ethnic-minority populations: a Community Guide systematic review. American Journal of Preventive Medicine, 46(3), 312–323.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.amepre.2013.12.003
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract only (PubMed 24512872; not in PMC). The confidence interval is 0.06 to 0.43 for math and 0.32 to 0.61 for verbal. These pooled figures are the review's own synthesis and are larger than the "about one quarter" in the Cooper abstract. Both are effects at the end of kindergarten (or the start of first grade).

4. Five Indiana school districts used lotteries in 2007–08 to fill a limited number of full-day kindergarten seats (23 schools). Hispanic students gained 0.70 standard deviations, and children who entered with low literacy skills also gained more. The study measured only the end of the kindergarten year.
   - citation: Gibbs, C. R. (2014). Experimental Evidence on Early Intervention: The Impact of Full-day Kindergarten. Working paper, September 2014.
   - source: https://economics.nd.edu/assets/141429/
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract and study-design sections (PDF pp. 2 and 11–17). The five "districts" are four districts and one charter school operating as its own district. The author describes this as the first evidence based on random assignment to kindergarten type. Unpublished working paper. raw/research/fdk-gibbs-nd-2014.pdf

5. A RAND study used a national sample of U.S. children who started kindergarten in 1998–99 (the Early Childhood Longitudinal Study). Full-day kindergarten predicted reading scores 0.145 standard deviations higher and math scores 0.119 standard deviations higher at the end of kindergarten. By the end of first grade, the math effect was cut in half and the reading effect was gone; by third grade, the math effect was no longer statistically significant.    - citation: Cannon, J. S., Jacknowitz, A., & Painter, G. (2005). Is Full Better than Half? Examining the Longitudinal Effects of Full-day Kindergarten Attendance. RAND Labor and Population Working Paper WR-266-1. (Published 2006 in Journal of Policy Analysis and Management, 25(2), 299–321, https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.20174.)
   - source: https://www.rand.org/dam/rand/pubs/working_papers/2005/RAND_WR266-1.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08

## research-class-size: Do Smaller Classes Help?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What strong studies find about the effect of smaller classes on students' learning and later life.
- summary: The strongest U.S. evidence, Tennessee's Project STAR experiment (1985–89), found that students randomly placed in classes of about 15 instead of about 22 in kindergarten through third grade scored about 4 to 5 percentile points higher, with larger gains for minority and low-income students and small later increases in college-going. Other credible studies disagree: a Swedish study found lasting gains into adult wages, while studies in Connecticut, Florida's statewide mandate and recent Israeli data found effects near zero, and a 2018 international systematic review concluded the effect is at best small for reading and not statistically significant for math. Results appear to depend on how large the reduction is, which grades and students are involved, and whether enough experienced teachers can be hired, so how much a particular reduction would help is uncertain.

1. A 2018 Campbell Collaboration systematic review found 127 studies of class size from 41 countries but could use only 10 in its meta-analysis. For the studies outside Tennessee's STAR experiment, smaller classes had a small positive effect on reading (standardized mean difference 0.11, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.16) and a negative but not statistically significant effect on math (−0.03, 95% CI −0.22 to 0.16), measured at the end of the school year. The four STAR analyses, kept separate, all showed positive effects (0.17 to 0.34 in reading and 0.15 to 0.33 in math). The authors concluded that the evidence points to "no or only very small" effects, and that they cannot rule out that small classes are counterproductive for some students.
   - citation: Filges, T., Sonne-Schmidt, C. S., & Nielsen, B. C. V. (2018). Small class sizes for improving student achievement in primary and secondary schools: a systematic review. Campbell Systematic Reviews, 2018:10.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.4073/csr.2018.10
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read the full executive summary with results (Europe PMC record for PMC8428040); the Wiley PDF was blocked by a bot check. Searches ran to February 2017 and covered kindergarten–grade 12. The non-STAR studies in the meta-analysis were from the USA, the Netherlands and France; 58 studies were excluded for high risk of bias. The overall reading effect equals a 53% chance that a random small-class student outscores a random larger-class student.

2. In Tennessee's Project STAR experiment, about 11,600 students and their teachers were randomly assigned to small or regular-size classes from kindergarten through third grade (79 schools, 1985–89). On average, students' standardized test scores rose by about four percentile points in the first year they attended a small class, and the advantage grew by about one percentile point per year after that. The effect was larger for minority students and students on free lunch, while teacher aides in regular classes had little effect.
   - citation: Krueger, A. B. (1999). Experimental Estimates of Education Production Functions. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 114(2), 497–532.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1162/003355399556052
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract only (via OpenAlex). The NBER Working Paper 6051 PDF is a scan whose text could not be extracted, and the journal is paywalled. The number of schools and years come from Chetty et al. (2011), which also reports that small classes averaged 15.1 students and large classes 22.6, with a 4.8 percentile-point gain in the entry year.

3. A follow-up of Project STAR students using college records found that assignment to a small class in kindergarten through third grade increased the probability of attending college by 2.7 percentage points. The effect was more than twice as large for Black students, and 7.3 percentage points for students in the poorest third of schools. Small classes increased the chance of earning a college degree by 1.6 percentage points and shifted students toward high-earning fields such as science, technology, engineering, math, business and economics.
   - citation: Dynarski, S., Hyman, J. M., & Schanzenbach, D. W. (2013). Experimental Evidence on the Effect of Childhood Investments on Postsecondary Attainment and Degree Completion. Journal of Policy Analysis and Management, 32(4), 692–717.
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w17533
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read NBER Working Paper 17533 (revised July 2013), abstract and results; published version https://doi.org/10.1002/pam.21715. The 1.6-point degree effect is statistically significant only at the 10% level (PDF p. 16 of the working paper). raw/research/classsize-dynarski-nber-w17533.pdf

4. A study linking Project STAR students to tax records found that students assigned to small classes were 1.8 percentage points more likely to be enrolled in college at age 20 (against an average of 26.4%). It found no significant difference in earnings at age 27, but the earnings estimate was imprecise, so a gain of up to about 3.4% a year could not be ruled out.
   - citation: Chetty, R., Friedman, J. N., Hilger, N., Saez, E., Schanzenbach, D. W., & Yagan, D. (2011). How Does Your Kindergarten Classroom Affect Your Earnings? Evidence from Project STAR. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 126(4), 1593–1660.
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w16381
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read NBER Working Paper 16381 (revised August 2011), pp. 2–4 and 16–18; published version https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjr041. The college effect has p = 0.06. The earnings point estimate with controls was −$124 (SE $336), with an upper 95% bound of $535 (3.4%). Small-class students also scored 4.6% of a standard deviation higher, a significant difference, on a summary index of adult outcomes (home ownership, 401(k) savings, marriage, mobility, neighborhood). raw/research/classsize-chetty-nber-w16381.pdf

5. A study in Sweden used a rule capping classes at 30 students for children aged 10 to 13 (cohorts born 1967, 1972, 1977 and 1982). It found that smaller classes raised cognitive and non-cognitive test scores at age 13 and cognitive scores at 16 and 18, without substantial fade-out, and increased completed education and wages at ages 27 to 42. Each one-pupil reduction in class size raised adult wages by 0.7 percent, with the effect concentrated among people whose parents had above-median income.
   - citation: Fredriksson, P., Öckert, B., & Oosterbeek, H. (2013). Long-Term Effects of Class Size. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 128(1), 249–285.
   - source: https://docs.iza.org/dp5879.pdf
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read IZA Discussion Paper 5879 (July 2011), abstract and results. The published version (https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjs048) was not opened, so its numbers may differ. Regression discontinuity design. For people with high-income parents, the wage gain was 1.3 percent per pupil. raw/research/classsize-fredriksson-iza-dp5879.pdf

6. A 1996 California law cut class sizes in kindergarten through 3rd grade by roughly ten students per class. A study of third graders found that each one-student reduction raised the share scoring above the national median by about 0.4 points in math and 0.3 points in reading, or about 4 and 3 percentage points for the ten-student drop. But the rapid hiring of new, less experienced teachers lowered teacher quality, especially in schools serving mostly Black students, which partly, and in some cases fully, offset the benefits.
   - citation: Jepsen, C., & Rivkin, S. (2002). What is the Tradeoff Between Smaller Classes and Teacher Quality? NBER Working Paper 9205. (Published 2009 as "Class Size Reduction and Student Achievement: The Potential Tradeoff between Teacher Quality and Class Size," Journal of Human Resources, 44(1), 223–250.)
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w9205
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08

Not randomized: the study uses year-to-year enrollment variation and program timing. raw/research/classsize-jepsen-rivkin-nber-w9205.pdf

7. Florida's 2002 constitutional amendment capped class sizes (18 in pre-K–3, 22 in grades 4–8 and 25 in high school, by 2010–11), at an estimated cost of about $20 billion over eight years. A study compared districts required to reduce class size with districts that got equal money without the requirement, and found little, if any, effect. After three years, math scores were a statistically insignificant 0.035 standard deviations higher, and reading was no higher. Over those three years, class size in the required districts fell only about 1.5 students more than in comparison districts.
   - citation: Chingos, M. M. (2012). The impact of a universal class-size reduction policy: Evidence from Florida's statewide mandate. Economics of Education Review, 31(5), 543–562.
   - source: https://web.archive.org/web/20170703223239/https://www.hks.harvard.edu/pepg/PDF/Papers/PEPG10-03_Chingos.pdf

   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read Harvard PEPG Working Paper 10-03 (revised August 2010): abstract, pp. 3 and 18–19, and conclusions. The published version (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2012.03.002) was not opened. 8. A study of 649 elementary schools used natural year-to-year swings in the number of children in each grade, along with class-size rules.    - citation: Hoxby, C. M. (2000). The Effects of Class Size on Student Achievement: New Evidence from Population Variation. Quarterly Journal of Economics, 115(4), 1239–1285.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1162/003355300555060
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract only (via OpenAlex). The NBER Working Paper 6869 PDF has no text layer, and the journal is paywalled. ## research-district-consolidation: Does Merging Small Districts or Schools Save Money or Change Results?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What studies of school district mergers and school consolidations find about costs and student results, and what is still unknown.
- summary: Studies of school district mergers come from other states and countries, and their findings depend on the setting. A study of rural New York districts estimated large operating-cost savings when very small districts merge, smaller once transition and building costs are counted and smallest for districts near 1,500 students; studies of Iowa and Arkansas mergers found no clear savings, while a North Carolina study found lower per-pupil spending on instruction and district support after mergers. Effects of district mergers on students have generally been small or near zero (Arkansas, North Carolina, the Netherlands), but closing or merging schools brought a short-term dip in test scores in Denmark, and a national study of the 1930–1970 consolidation era linked larger schools to lower adult wages. No study of consolidation in New Hampshire itself was found, and most of the studies here could be read only in abstract form.

1. A study of rural school districts in New York estimated economies of size in operating spending: all else equal, doubling enrollment cuts operating costs per pupil by 61.7 percent for a 300-pupil district and by 49.6 percent for a 1,500-pupil district. Merging also brought large adjustment costs, especially for capital spending, which lowered the net savings to 31.5 percent for a 300-pupil district and 14.4 percent for a 1,500-pupil district. The authors concluded that consolidation makes fiscal sense, particularly for very small districts, but that states should avoid subsidizing unwarranted capital projects.

## research-career-technical-education: Do Career and Technical Education Students Do Better After High School?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What strong studies find about high school career and technical education (CTE) and students' graduation, college-going, jobs and earnings.
- summary: A 2024 federally funded review of 28 causal studies found that high school CTE, on average, raised high school completion, academic achievement, employment after high school and enrollment in two-year colleges, with no significant average effect on four-year college enrollment, degree completion or earnings (only two of its studies measured earnings). ## research-dual-enrollment: Does Taking College Courses in High School Help Students Finish College?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What strong studies find about dual enrollment and early college high schools and later college enrollment and degree completion.
- summary: Lottery-based studies of early college high schools (small whole-school programs where students take a planned sequence of college courses) find that more students earn a college degree, mostly associate degrees: 6 to 10 years after high school the gap in earning any degree was 8.5 to 11.3 percentage points, while bachelor's degree rates for the comparison students caught up over time. For the more common model of taking individual college courses in high school, studies comparing participants with similar students consistently find higher college enrollment and completion, and the federal What Works Clearinghouse rates dual enrollment as having positive effects on degree attainment. These comparison studies cannot fully rule out that more motivated students choose to take the courses, and the one randomized trial of a single dual-credit course (a Tennessee math course) found no change in overall college enrollment. No study of the effects of New Hampshire's own programs, such as Running Start, was found; the only New Hampshire figures are descriptive.

1. The U.S. Department of Education's What Works Clearinghouse (WWC) reviewed dual enrollment research in 2017 and found five studies that met its standards, together covering 77,249 high school students across the United States. Two were randomized trials of early college high schools, and three compared participants with matched non-participants (one national, two in Texas). The WWC rated dual enrollment as having "positive effects" on college degree attainment, college access and enrollment, credit accumulation, completing high school, and general academic achievement in high school, each with a "medium to large" extent of evidence. On the WWC's improvement index (the expected change in an average student's percentile rank), the average effect was +25 percentile points for degree attainment (range +6 to +42 across the five studies) and +15 for college enrollment. It found "no discernible effects" on general academic achievement in college, based on one study of 455 students.

## research-chronic-absence: What Happens When Students Are Chronically Absent, and What Helps?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What careful studies find about how missed school days affect learning and graduation, and which efforts to raise attendance have been tested.
- summary: Careful studies find that missed school lowers learning a little for each day missed, and the harm adds up: in one large California district, missing 10 classes in middle or high school lowered test scores by 3–4% of a standard deviation, and 10 absences in 9th grade lowered on-time graduation and college enrollment by about 2%. The harm appears in elementary school too, and in North Carolina it was larger for low-income students and for unexcused absences. Randomized trials show that low-cost mailings telling parents how many days their child has missed cut chronic absence by roughly 10–15% (one result fell just short of statistical significance), and a mentoring program in Chicago cut absences for students in grades 5–7 but not grades 1–4. Reviews find that most attendance programs have small effects or have not been rigorously tested, and the Chicago mentoring trial found no significant effect on test scores or grades.

1. A study of students in grades 7–11 in a large urban school district in California (school years 2002-03 through 2012-13) compared each student's absences in different classes. It found that missing 10 classes lowered math or English language arts test scores by 3–4% of a standard deviation, and course grades by 17–18% of a standard deviation. Ten total absences across all subjects in 9th grade lowered both the chance of graduating on time and the chance of ever enrolling in college by 2%. The authors describe these as causal effects, and absences after the state testing window did not affect test scores, which supports that reading.
   - citation: Liu, J., Lee, M., & Gershenson, S. (2021). The short- and long-run impacts of secondary school absences. Journal of Public Economics, 199, 104441.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpubeco.2021.104441
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Effect sizes are from the published abstract (IDEAS/RePEc record https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/pubeco/v199y2021ics0047272721000773.html, checked twice); the full journal text is paywalled. The setting (district, 2002-03 to 2012-13, grades 7–11) comes from the 2019 working paper (IZA DP 12613, pp. 4–6 and the Table 1 note; raw/research/absence-liu-lee-gershenson-iza12613.pdf). The working paper reported larger numbers (7% of an SD for math scores, an 8% lower chance of on-time graduation, 7% for college), so use the published figures.

2. A study looked at kindergarten and first-grade students in a national sample (the federal Early Childhood Longitudinal Study, kindergarten class of 1998-99) and 4th and 5th graders in North Carolina public schools (2005-06 to 2009-10). It found that absences were linked to "modest but statistically significant" drops in achievement. Each additional absence was associated with scores 0.002 standard deviations lower in math and in reading in kindergarten and first grade. In North Carolina's 4th and 5th grades, the figures were 0.007 standard deviations in math and 0.004 in reading. The harm grew roughly in step with days missed and was similar in urban, suburban and rural schools. In North Carolina it was larger for low-income students and English learners, and unexcused absences were twice as harmful as excused ones. This study measures associations, after accounting for students' prior scores and their classroom; it is not a randomized experiment.

   - as_of: 2026-11-08

## research-cell-phone-restrictions: Do School Cell Phone Restrictions Affect Learning or Well-Being?

- kind: research
- question_type: research
- description: What studies find about school phone bans and restrictions and students' test scores, discipline and well-being. The evidence is limited and mixed.
- summary: The evidence is thin and mixed. There are no randomized trials; studies compare schools that restricted phones with schools that did not, so their results are less certain. Some find small test-score gains (England's earlier bans; a large Florida district in the second year of its ban, about 1 percentile on spring tests), while others find none (Sweden) or an average near zero (a 2026 national U.S. study of lockable phone pouches). Effects on well-being are also mixed: an English study found no difference in mental well-being, Norway found benefits for girls, and both U.S. studies found more discipline in the first year that faded later; long-run effects are unknown.

1. A study of high schools in four English cities (Birmingham, Leicester, London and Manchester) compared national exam (GCSE) results before and after each school banned phones. It used exam data from 2001 to 2011 and a 2013 survey of schools (91 schools responded). After a ban, students' test scores rose by 6.41% of a standard deviation. The gain came from the lowest-achieving students, whose scores rose 14.23% of a standard deviation, while students in the top quarter were neither helped nor hurt. There were no significant gains where a ban was not widely followed.
   - citation: Beland, L.-P., & Murphy, R. (2016). Ill Communication: Technology, distraction & student performance. Labour Economics, 41, 61–76.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.labeco.2016.04.004
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: The figures are from the May 2015 working paper (CEP Discussion Paper 1350, pp. 2–3; raw/research/phones-beland-murphy-cep1350.pdf), not the journal version; check them against the published article. The design compares schools that chose to ban phones at different times (difference-in-differences). The survey response rate was 21%.

2. A Swedish study used the same approach as the English one, but with nearly all of Sweden's grade 9 schools: 1,086 of 1,423 schools answered a 2018-19 survey about whether and when they banned phones, and about 60% had a ban by 2017. It found "no impact of mobile phone bans on student performance," and could rule out even small gains.
   - citation: Kessel, D., Hardardottir, H. L., & Tyrefors, B. (2020). The impact of banning mobile phones in Swedish secondary schools. Economics of Education Review, 77, 102009.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.econedurev.2020.102009
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Read in the working-paper version (IFN Working Paper 1288, revised February 2020; raw/research/phones-kessel-hardardottir-tyrefors-ifn1288.pdf): abstract and pp. 4, 7–8. The authors suggest that Swedish teachers may already restrict phones in class whatever the school's policy.

3. A study of Norwegian middle schools tracked how outcomes changed around the time each school banned smartphones, using administrative and survey data. It found no average effect on education or mental health, but differences between girls and boys. The bans reduced girls' use of psychological health care and lowered bullying for both girls and boys. They also improved girls' grade point averages and placement in academic high school tracks, especially for girls from disadvantaged backgrounds.
   - citation: Abrahamsson, S. (2026). Smartphone Bans, Student Outcomes and Mental Health. Journal of Human Resources (published online March 2026).
   - source: https://doi.org/10.3368/jhr.0224-13403R2
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Abstract only (journal page https://jhr.uwpress.org/node/16076). The full text is paywalled and the NHH working paper repository was unreachable, so no effect sizes are given. The design is an event study, not randomized.

4. A study of a large urban county school district in Florida, one of the ten largest in the U.S., looked at a rule, starting in 2023-24, that phones be silenced and put away for the whole school day. It compared schools where students had used phones more or less before the ban. In the first year, suspensions rose: overall suspension rates by 12% and in-school suspensions by roughly 20%, with in-school suspensions for Black students up by about 30%. Suspensions mostly returned to earlier levels in the second year (2024-25). Test-score gains came mainly in the second year. Average scores rose about 0.6 percentiles, and spring test scores rose 1.1 percentiles (1.4 for male students and 1.3 in middle and high schools), with no significant effect for female students or elementary students. Unexcused absences fell, mostly in middle and high schools, and the authors estimate this explains nearly half of the test-score gain there.
   - citation: Figlio, D. N., & Özek, U. (2025). The Impact of Cellphone Bans in Schools on Student Outcomes: Evidence from Florida. NBER Working Paper No. 34388.
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w34388
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Full text read (raw/research/phones-figlio-ozek-florida-nber34388.pdf): abstract and pp. 5–6, 16–17. Not peer reviewed. The design is quasi-experimental (difference-in-differences using building-level phone activity data from before the ban). In the first year, middle and high schools also saw a 0.7 percentile gain (p. 16). The district is not named.

5. A 2026 national U.S. study compared middle and high schools that adopted lockable phone pouches (Yondr) in the 2022-23, 2023-24 or 2024-25 school years with similar schools that didn't. The pouches greatly reduced phone use: teachers reported that the share of students using phones in class for personal reasons fell from 61% to 13%. Average effects on test scores were "consistently close to zero," with modest gains in high school math (about 0.024 standard deviations, roughly 0.9 percentile points) and small losses in middle schools. Discipline rose in the first year (roughly a 15% increase in suspension rates) and then faded. Student-reported well-being fell by about 0.2 standard deviations in the first year and then rose by 0.16 standard deviations by the second year, which the authors read with caution. Effects on attendance and chronic absence were close to zero, and there was no clear improvement in classroom attention or online bullying.
   - citation: Allcott, H., Baron, E. J., Dee, T., Duckworth, A. L., Gentzkow, M., & Jacob, B. (2026). The Effects of School Phone Bans: National Evidence from Lockable Pouches. NBER Working Paper No. 35132.
   - source: https://www.nber.org/papers/w35132
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Full text read (April 2026, revised September 2026; raw/research/phones-allcott-et-al-pouches-nber35132.pdf): abstract and pp. 2–4 and 10–13. Not peer reviewed. The design is staggered difference-in-differences. The paper labels school years by their spring year (its "2023–2025" means 2022-23 through 2024-25). Middle school test scores come from 22 states' school-level data plus the Stanford Education Data Archive (SEDA). Outcomes cover at most three years after adoption.

6. A study of 1,227 students aged 12–15 in 30 English secondary schools (20 that didn't allow recreational phone use and 10 that did), with data collected between November 2022 and November 2023, found no difference in mental well-being between the two groups: an adjusted difference of −0.48 points on a well-being scale averaging 47 (95% confidence interval −2.05 to 1.06). Students in restrictive schools used phones about 0.67 hours less and social media about 0.54 hours less during the school day, but their total phone and social media time on weekdays and weekends didn't differ. Sleep, physical activity, academic attainment and classroom behavior also didn't differ. Because the study looked at one point in time, it shows associations, not cause and effect.
   - citation: Goodyear, V. A., Randhawa, A., Adab, P., Al-Janabi, H., Fenton, S., Jones, K., Michail, M., Morrison, B., Patterson, P., Quinlan, J., Sitch, A., Twardochleb, R., Wade, M., & Pallan, M. (2025). School phone policies and their association with mental wellbeing, phone use, and social media use (SMART Schools): a cross-sectional observational study. The Lancet Regional Health – Europe, 51, 101211.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lanepe.2025.101211
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Full text read via PubMed Central (PMC11984610). Well-being was measured with the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-Being Scale. The authors note the risk of selection bias and unmeasured confounding.

7. A 2024 review that combined five quantitative studies of school smartphone bans (including studies from Norway, Spain, England and Sweden) found a small overall effect (d = 0.162 in the abstract; 0.15 in the results text). The effect was larger for social well-being, such as less bullying (d = 0.22, statistically significant), than for academic performance (d = 0.05, not statistically significant). The authors say the small number of studies limits the conclusions.
   - citation: Böttger, T., & Zierer, K. (2024). To Ban or Not to Ban? A Rapid Review on the Impact of Smartphone Bans in Schools on Social Well-Being and Academic Performance. Education Sciences, 14(8), 906.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.3390/educsci14080906
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
   - note: Full text read (raw/research/phones-bottger-zierer-2024-rapid-review.pdf): abstract, Table 1 and pp. 6–8. The abstract (d = 0.162) and the results text (d = 0.15) give different overall figures. The review uses an earlier working-paper version of Abrahamsson, and predates the Florida and national U.S. studies. The fifth study (Cakirpaloglu et al., 2020) is listed without a country, because its country wasn't checked. The authors' policy recommendation is left out on purpose.

8. A 2024 scoping review of the worldwide research on phones in schools found 22 studies on academic outcomes, mental health and well-being, and cyberbullying. It found no randomized controlled trials. It said the evidence rests on a small number of studies with different designs, samples, outcome measures and definitions of a ban (partial or complete), which makes the findings hard to reconcile.
   - citation: Campbell, M. A., Edwards, E. J., Pennell, D., Poed, S., Lister, V. P. M., Gillett-Swan, J. K., Kelly, A., Zec, D., & Nguyen, T.-A. (2024). Evidence for and against banning mobile phones in schools: A scoping review. Journal of Psychologists and Counsellors in Schools, 34, 242–265.
   - source: https://doi.org/10.1177/20556365241270394
   - as_of: 2026-11-08
   - review_by: 2027-11-08
